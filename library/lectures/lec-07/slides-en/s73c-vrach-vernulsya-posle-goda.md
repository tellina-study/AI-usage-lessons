## Visible slide text
**Here, what gets measured is the performer himself: is he in form.** Skill is spent in silence.

**An adenoma is a precancerous thickening of the mucosa: removed today, it will not become cancer years from now.**

**Data is gathered four ways, and all four run on the ordinary flow of work:** an audit of the decisions made on the job — the rate of procedures with a finding · timing by stopwatch · a count of procedures for the year · periodic re-certification. **Case review comes after the outcome and is not part of the count.**

**The finding rate cannot be raised by trying harder:** an adenoma lands in the denominator even when the physician did not look behind the fold. Withdrawal time is a discipline — easier to keep up.

**The decision:** authorize, narrow the authorization, suspend it, or not renew it.

**Why this stage stands here:** erosion accumulates across all stages at once — it can only be gathered once all of them have passed.

**Of the four methods, one measures the result; the rest measure behavior and volume. Which one — and why is it the easiest to substitute with the other two?**

## Speaker notes

This stage is built differently from all the previous ones, and that distinction has to come first. Every previous section measured the work: the stream of alerts, the document, the scan, the procedure around the signature. Here the object being measured is the performer himself, and there is one question for him: is he staying in form. An engineer is used to thinking of qualification as something issued once: a diploma earned, an authorization completed, a certificate in hand. In medicine it works differently, and this carries the whole section: a physician's skill is a consumable.

Now, what data about the person does this stage gather, and how. There are four methods, and all four run on the ordinary flow of work, with no separate trial.

An audit of the decisions made on completed work is the detection rate, and more on that separately below. The volume-of-practice count comes from the log of completed work: skill at a manual procedure is maintained by performing it, and from the outside this is checked by one quantity — how many times the person performed it. Professional societies set the minimum number per year, and below it a specialist is considered to have fallen out of form.

The detection rate is counted from ordinary reports, with no separate trial at all — and that is a rare property: the profession gets a check on an individual performer's condition straight out of his everyday work, with no exam set up for him. The rate is built cleverly. It is the share of procedures in which the physician found at least one precancerous growth, out of all his screening procedures, and it cannot be gotten by trying hard, by willpower, or by careful wording in the report: a growth that requires looking behind a fold lands in the denominator regardless of whether the physician looked, and lands in the numerator only if he did. So the detection rate measures behavior, not reporting about it.

Withdrawal time is timed with a stopwatch: the examination is done during withdrawal of the endoscope, and the slower the withdrawal, the more gets seen in time, with the lower bound set by guidelines. The fourth method is periodic re-certification: it gathers both accumulated study and accumulated practice, because between two re-certifications a specialist's condition changes in both directions. Case review is not among these four: it comes AFTER the outcome, that is, it checks an error that has already happened, and the silent drain of skill never reaches it. There is no fifth method — a trial with the assistant switched off — in any jurisdiction.

What decision this stage makes. Authorize · narrow the authorization to part of the procedures · suspend it · not renew it. Behind this decision stands everything that came before: the person either continues to decide at the earlier stages — calling patients in for examination, reading a measurement, making a diagnosis, signing — or he does not. And this is the one stage all evening whose decision is not about the patient.

And why this stage's measurement is taken off the person as a whole, across all stages at once, rather than being set as a step inside the cycle. The previous section ended with a signature that may have nothing behind it: the cost of error moves onto someone who did not sign it. Here the same thought turns to face the person as a whole. The one who signs is the one person all evening that no measurement has measured: the work has been measured the whole lecture, and nobody has measured the one who remains the decisive link inside that work. This cannot be placed as a step alongside selection, diagnostics, and observation: a work-step does not show a measurement like this at all, and lining up nine stages in a row would mean pretending they are uniform.

One caveat about the order. This stage stands last among the substantive ones all evening not because it is less important, but because a person's erosion can only be measured after it has been shown what he was doing and what held him in place. Put it earlier, and there would be nothing to measure.

**Sources.** [colonoscopy quality indicators, Gastrointestinal Endoscopy 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gie.2014.07.058)

And the same field with the model, named before the breakdown. Of the eight actions, the trained model took over one — the withdrawal examination, where a box appears in real time on the relevant part of the image. One step was added: deciding whether to trust that box. Preparation, inserting the scope, the fold, removing what's found, histology, the report, and tallying the physician's own metrics stayed with people and the profession. And the three places where the model does not work are named by what is missing there: it cannot maintain skill — the direct opposite is measured; it has nothing to show the physician that he would not have seen without highlighting inside the procedure itself; and it cannot measure his own result without the assistant, and neither can any procedure in any jurisdiction. The direction the tool is developing in is worth noting right away: from detection it is moving toward characterizing what is found, and further toward automatically tallying the physician's own metrics — that is, it is also becoming a measuring instrument for the person who uses it.

The stage's eight actions in order: prepare the patient · insert the scope to the cecum · examine during withdrawal · check behind the fold · remove what's found · send it for histology · write up the report · tally the physician's own metrics. The physician does the first seven by hand; the profession does the eighth for him, per published guidelines — and this is the only place in the stage where someone other than the physician himself measures his form.

The two metrics of this outside check are built differently. The detection rate — the share of procedures in which at least one growth was found — is a quantity taken straight from the flow of work, and there is no way to raise it directly by trying: it is either there in the work, or it isn't. Withdrawal time is a disciplinary quantity: its lower bound is set by guidelines, and compliance is checked by a stopwatch. The first measures the result, the second measures behavior, and one must not be substituted for the other — though in practice it is: time is easier to hold to.
