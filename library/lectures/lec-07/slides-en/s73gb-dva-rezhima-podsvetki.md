## Visible slide text
**Both clinical breakdowns in this section are taken on one mode — highlighting. There are two modes, and the difference isn't in accuracy.**

**Video highlighting:** the physician performs the procedure, a box appears on the region, the spot stays with him — and the drain at that spot is measured. **Second reader of the scan:** the model takes the second person's spot, and the person is removed from that spot entirely.

**Skill is drained where the person formally stayed.** Where he was removed, the drain cannot be measured. This is a property of the decision itself, and no future measurement will close that gap.

## Speaker notes

One class of tool works in two modes, and the difference between them is what's left to the person. Both clinical breakdowns in this section, the Polish and the Russian, are taken on one of the two: video highlighting during a procedure, where the physician performs the procedure himself and a box appears on the region. The second mode removes the person from that spot. The consequences for the person differ between the two modes. If this isn't separated out now, the room will walk away from the lecture with the takeaway "AI drains skill," and the correct takeaway is different, and more complicated.

The first mode — video highlighting. The physician performs the procedure himself, a box appears in real time on the relevant part of the image, and he decides whether to trust it or not. The place of work stays with the person entirely: he inserts the scope, withdraws it, checks behind the fold, removes what's found. The tool did not take over a single one of these actions; it added a cue to one of them. And it is exactly at this spot that the drain on skill is measured: for the same nineteen physicians, in procedures without the assistant, the rate dropped from 284 findings per 1,000 procedures to 224, over three months. This is the breakdown we just went through.

The second mode — the second reader. The scan is already taken, a person reads it, and the program requires two independent readers. The model takes the second reader's spot and delivers its own interpretation. Here the person is removed from the spot entirely, and that is not a figure of speech: in the Swedish program, the volume of human reading turned out to be lower by 44.3 percent — this is an interim safety analysis, 80,033 screened. More than forty percent of the work people used to do, people stopped doing.

And now the reason this slide stands apart from the summary. The drain on skill at the second spot is not measured — and cannot be measured. Not because nobody took it on, and not because the measurement is hard: there's nobody there to measure. The person who would have read second, and whose skill could be compared "before" and "after," no longer exists at this spot. This is important to distinguish from every other gap we've named today. Those gaps are a property of our knowledge: there is no procedure for it, but one could be set up. This one is a property of the decision that was made: as long as the spot is occupied by the model, there is nothing to measure, and no future work will fix that.

From this comes a formulation worth stating verbatim: skill is drained where the person formally stayed. As long as he is at the spot and still doing the work, the volume of his own practice quietly declines, and that decline can be caught — the Polish study caught it. Where he was removed, there is nothing left to decline, and worrying about erosion there is pointless: what's worth worrying about instead is who will read if the model is recalled tomorrow, and where the people will come from who had nothing to train on. We already saw this question today, in cytology: the volume dropped, the centers were cut from forty-five to eight, there was nothing left to train on, and there was no model at all in that story.

Separately, about what this slide does not claim. It does not say the second mode is better or safer. It says that the cost of the two modes differs in nature, and it is measured in different quantities: for the first, by the finding rate for the same person; for the second, by how many people are left in the profession and what they're being trained on. These two costs cannot be compared by one number, and attempting such a comparison would be exactly what we've spent the whole evening examining other people's work for.

And last, before the stage summary. Next we will put the whole stage together — what works, what doesn't, what of this transfers to engineering work in general — and the distinction between the two modes is not repeated there: it is made here, and the summary rests on it.
