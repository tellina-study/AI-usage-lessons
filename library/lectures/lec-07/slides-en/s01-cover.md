## Visible slide text

# The decision about a person: who looks, who signs, who pays

### Lecture 7

Today, someone's scan will be read before a person ever looks at it.

## Speaker notes

The lecture examines medicine: eight stages of the care-delivery cycle, a trained model at each one, and the person who signs off after it. The decision made here is a decision about a person, and that is what sets the cost of error.

How the subject differs from the last lecture: there, what accumulated was counted in units of product; here, it is counted in people. A batch of defective parts gets recalled; a clinical decision, once made, cannot be recalled, even when the mistake is caught right after. That changes the very framing of the question of the cost of error, and the course moves into medicine second, so that the cost of refusal becomes visible by contrast with the industry already covered. The subject itself stays the same: the same pair of questions — what a miss costs, what a false alarm costs — the same method of analysis through the cost of both errors. The boundary of acceptable risk is drawn in a different place, and abstract reasoning does not show that.

What "the procedure holds down the cost of error" means: double reading of a scan, a colleague's second opinion, a case conference for a disputed case, informed consent before an intervention — not one of these devices was invented for artificial intelligence. All of them existed long before it and solved one problem: so that one person's mistake would not become the final decision without a check. The model enters a procedure that is already in place; it does not build one of its own. That is why the lecture's question is about the procedure itself and about the person inside it: what happens to them when a participant appears beside them who does not get tired, does not doubt, and does not ask for a pause.

What is worth taking away, even if every number from tonight is forgotten by the end: the question has to be asked concretely — what exactly does the person do, in each specific case, after the model has already looked. The lecture spends the whole evening answering exactly that question, case by case. It does not hand out grades to the tools — it shows what each tool did to the work around it.
