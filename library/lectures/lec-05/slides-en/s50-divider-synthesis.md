# Visible content

## Section divider
**SECTION 7**

## Synthesis: one instrument for all six phases

## Tag
6 phases · 1 matrix · 3 decision axes

## Why this section is needed
The six phases were examined one at a time, and a task arrives whole — nobody will break it down for you along the lecture's sections. Without a common instrument you are left with six separate conclusions and no answer for a case that was not covered here.

## Speaker notes

Section seven closes the lecture. It is not here to repeat what we have already covered: repetition adds nothing and costs time. It is here because in this lecture we solved one and the same task six times in pieces, and at work it arrives whole. A client does not say "I have a question about the measurement phase" — the client says "we want the model to answer customers on its own." Answering that takes an instrument that works on any phase, and on a case you have never seen before.

Over the lecture we went through discovery, design, build and launch, measurement, support, and governance. The same structure repeated on every phase: there is a classical discipline, there is a specific piece of work that artificial intelligence made cheaper, there is a way of letting you down characteristic of that phase, and there is a point where the decision is not handed to a tool. As long as those four things are named six times separately, they stay six stories. Assembled onto one screen, they become an apparatus you can use.

Two instruments follow in this section. The first is a summary table: six phase rows against four columns, where every cell is derived from material already covered and none was assigned after the fact. You can argue with any of its rows only by arguing with the matching section of the lecture. The second is three axes: reversibility, the cost of an error and observability. They give the phase its operating mode by a single rule, and they are tried on two cases we have already examined — an instrument has to be shown working, declaring that it works is not enough. The order matters here: first the table answers the question "what is actually going on in this phase," and only then the three axes answer "which mode is permissible here." The order is easy to swap, and then the decision about how much autonomy to grant is taken before the nature of the phase is understood.

The section closes with a bridge in two directions: to the seminar, where you will walk the same loop on your own practice task, and to the next lecture, where the same method meets material with a higher cost of physical error. One thought for the whole section: there will be no new facts from here on — there will be a way to apply the ones you already have.
