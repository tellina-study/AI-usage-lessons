## Visible slide text

**Gave:** proof that AI hardware physically works and answers real requests in space.

**Required:** solving problems that don't exist on Earth — radiation hardening, heat rejection in vacuum with no convection, servicing with no human access — and a willingness to test some of the solutions only in actual flight.

## Speaker notes

Gave: proof that AI hardware physically works and answers real requests in space, not just in theoretical calculations. Before Starcloud-1's launch in November 2025, the claim "a data-center-class GPU can work and serve real requests in open space" hadn't been confirmed by a single flown system.

Required: solving problems that simply don't exist on Earth — radiation hardening of the electronics, heat rejection in vacuum with no air convection, servicing hardware with no human access — and an industry-wide acknowledged need to test some of these solutions only in real orbital conditions.

In sum: the start works — the hardware flies and answers requests — but the scale at which this becomes a competitive alternative to ground-based data centers remains a question of many years ahead, and that's honestly named through the specific launch-cost threshold from Section 0.

For Starcloud and Suncatcher, the first selection condition holds in its purest form — without hardware in orbit, the very fact of "computation in space" wouldn't exist at all. The second condition holds unconditionally — both cases are industry cases. The third is the weakest point of both breakdowns: there's no classical mode to compare against, and the numerical takeaway is limited to the fact that "the device works and answers requests." Both cases were brought into the lecture not despite this weakness, but with it explicitly named.
