## Speaker notes

The third breakdown shows a case that does not occur in the countable sections: a classical mode for this task does not exist at all, and the reason is named.

In money, there is no price for either error here, and there cannot be. The outcome is binary: either the role sounds in the actor's own voice, or it does not sound at all. This is a property of the case, not a gap in the measurement, and the frame states it exactly that way. The difference between "we could not calculate this" and "there is nothing to calculate here" matters: the first has to be closed by more searching, the second has to be said out loud and left behind.

A classical mode for this task does not exist at all, and the reason is medical. Val Kilmer's voice was lost after a tracheotomy for throat cancer in 2014. That leaves exactly two alternatives: a role with no voice, or a role with someone else's voice. Both give a different outcome instead of a different price, so there is no unit in which to add them up alongside the model. The course counts this as a full-fledged verdict; it is not treated as a gap.

What the model did. The work drew on an archive of the actor's recordings. The data came to roughly a tenth of what a project of this kind usually has — the algorithms had to be adapted for that. More than forty voice models were generated, and one was chosen from among them. Kilmer himself said of the result that his voice had been restored in a way he had not thought possible.

Notice the shape of the number "forty models, one chosen." This is exactly the same mechanics that, further on, gives 400 generations for 15 edits and 70,000 clips for a dozen-and-a-half shots: the win is drawn from the fact that a failed attempt costs almost nothing. In 2021, in sound, it already cost little enough to run through forty variants of one person's voice and pick the best. What is worth remembering is exactly this: where a trial is free, the work moves into selection, and this observation recurs three more times in the lecture.

How this works. Input is an archive of the actor's voice recordings, output is synthesized speech for the 2022 film "Top Gun: Maverick." The product's name is Sonantic, a company acquired by Spotify in 2022; the collaboration with the actor began in 2021. The training data was the actor's own archive, with his explicit consent.

What is missing. Exactly how many hours of archive were used is not stated: "a tenth of the usual" is not a number, and what counted as a usual project is not said either. No technical publications on the project could be found: the company was absorbed, and its blog is closed. This is a state of "searched in good faith and did not find," and it is flagged as such.

What moved. The precedent moved. A voice model exists and outlives the person: Kilmer died in 2025, and the model remains. Who controls it going forward, and under what terms, is decided by contract, because there is no right governing this.

And alongside it — a 2021 control point that supplies the one distinguishing factor that actually works. In the documentary "Roadrunner," the model spoke three lines that Anthony Bourdain wrote but never recorded; this came out from an interview with the director, and the film itself does not flag it in any way. The technology there and here is the same. There is exactly one difference: whether consent was declared or not. The same distinguishing factor operates in the previous breakdown, where a person clones their own voice and confirms it with a voice captcha; in the case of Darth Vader's voice, where rights were transferred during the actor's lifetime and explicitly; and in the case that closes this section, where there was no consent to synthesis in the contract at all. The ready-made criterion that carries over for an engineer: the question "was a model used" is useless — the working question is whose consent was obtained, and whether it is fixed in a document.
