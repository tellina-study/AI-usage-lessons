# 5. Professional qualification: maintenance and erosion

**What happened to the ones who decide.** The lecture has been measuring the work. What happened to the person inside that work is told by three measurements — and each has its own weakness.

**3 breakdowns · 2 classical modes with numbers · a review of authorization across three countries**

**Example 1: developers on an unfamiliar library** — the second half of the pair, measured by lottery, and taken outside the clinic.

**Example 2: the rate fell without the assistant** — the only measurement of erosion on real clinical work.

**Example 3: ArtInCol, Russia — an assistant on the same procedure** — the same quantity, a stronger trial, the drain not measured.

## Speaker notes

Professional qualification: maintenance and erosion. This stage measures not the work but the person who remains the decision-maker inside it, and three measurements speak to that person — retention among developers, the rate of procedures with a finding for the same physicians after the assistant is switched off, and the same quantity in a Russian trial. Each has its own weakness.

The previous section ended with a signature that may have nothing behind it: the cost of error moves onto someone who did not sign it. Here the same thought turns to face the person as a whole: there is one more step that nobody has touched — the person himself who decides. The work has been measured all evening: the stream of alerts, the document, the scan. None of these measurements says anything about what happens to the one who remains the decisive link inside that work.

This section differs from the general conversation about technology's effect on people in that it carries numbers taken from real work, not speculation about the future or general anxieties. The numbers are few, and that is exactly why they are precious: a direct measurement of deskilling on real clinical work happened once, and it has not been repeated. The first breakdown is taken on developers, not in a clinic, and it is randomized — stronger by design, but its magnitude cannot be carried over to physicians. The section states both of these limitations directly, without hiding or softening them.

There are three breakdowns in the section, and each does work the other two don't. First comes the randomized experiment on developers: it measures exactly the second half of the pair — the result without the assistant — and measures it by lottery, a method none of the section's clinical breakdowns has. It stands first for two reasons: it gives a yardstick for the grade of evidence before that yardstick gets used, and it is taken on the very work the room itself does. It is not clinical work, and the two grades must not be confused. Second comes the detection rate, measured on the same physicians before the assistant arrived and after a period of working with it: the only direct evidence of erosion on real clinical work. Third comes the Russian ArtInCol trial: it measures the same quantity on the same procedure with a stronger grade of evidence, but it has no observation period after the assistant is removed. A plane of "what about trust has not been measured" and a diagram with empty axes once stood here as separate slides and have been dropped by the owner; it is still worth saying that trust is not measured — but by voice, in the section's summary, not as its own slide. The new roles and steps that appeared once part of the decision came to be trusted to the model are gathered in the summary together with the rest.

That is why this stage comes last among the substantive ones: it carries through to the end the very thought the evening began with, and it does so by the same method the lecture uses to read other people's numbers — naming what is measured, and what is not.
