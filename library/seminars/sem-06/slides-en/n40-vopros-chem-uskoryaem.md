# What do we speed it up with?

## Assertion

Who here has already driven a task through all at once rather than one piece at a time — and with what exactly? Five means on the screen, and a separate worker is one of them. We pick a card and name the piece it works on.

> **Now the same on this task: pick a card — and name the piece your means works on.**

## Speaker notes

It is worth starting from your own. Who has already driven a task through all at once rather than one piece at a time — and with what exactly: several sessions, subagents, a check that runs itself? Whatever means gets named is then tested on the task we laid out: with your own means you can see which piece it worked on, and which piece there was simply nowhere to apply it to.

The task is laid out. What do we speed it up with?

The choice works like this: one card — and the piece the chosen means works on. A card without a piece does not count, because the whole choice lies in exactly that pair. "Subagents" with no piece named means "subagents everywhere", and the layout screen has just shown that everywhere does not work out for any of the five means.

Five means on the screen. The separate worker this rung is about is one of them, the third card from the left. The other four are older than it, and each is cheaper on one of the six pieces. Before reading on, it is worth picking a card and naming a piece, and naming a reason: what the chosen means wins by on that piece. The reason is half the value of the choice, because the mistake here almost always lies somewhere other than in the card, and it is visible only through the reason.

Which answers get said to this question more often than others, and what to do with each of them.

**"Subagents, and on all the pieces at once."** A strong and honest answer, and it is the one said most often. Arguing with it is premature: it is exactly what the breakdown will test. If it came to mind first, that is a good starting point, because the mistake in it does not lie where people usually look for it.

**"Narrow the task down."** An underrated move, and the only one of the five that removes work altogether instead of speeding it up. It has a condition of its own: narrowing can be done by whoever is answerable for the deadline. In most cases that is not the person doing the work, and then the move turns into a conversation with whoever set the task. That conversation costs less than two working weekends, which is why the move deserves to stand first in the list.

**"Do the pieces one at a time."** Defensible, and this answer has a real merit: the order is single and predictable, the pieces physically cannot collide with each other. It has one test question — at what number of pieces does it start to lose. The answer is in the arithmetic itself: the waits add up, and the more independent pieces there are in a task, the more expensive a queue turns out to be.

**"Run the pieces in several sessions."** A working move, and it has a precise place where it stumbles: what happens when two sessions reach the field and the error messages. Both pieces live in one file. That is a separate decision point with its own cost and its own defensive techniques, and it is examined later in the seminar; here it is enough to notice that the overlap on the file exists and that it decides the choice between this card and its neighbor.

**"Move the repeating part into a check that runs itself."** An answer that comes less often and lands precisely: the pre-release run stands in the task five times over, and there is nothing there for a human to do. The move has a condition too, and the breakdown will name it.

Picking two cards is not allowed, and that restriction is deliberate. A card plus a piece is already a full answer, whereas two cards mean two pieces, that is, two layouts at once, and the choice stops meaning anything: with two moves in reserve there will always be some piece on which at least one of them worked.
