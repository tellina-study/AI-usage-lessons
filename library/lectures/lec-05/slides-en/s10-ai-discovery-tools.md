# Visible content

## Title bar
What AI changed in research by 2026 — and what it did not

## Body
[A step-by-step comparison table → the scale "who is on the other end of the conversation" → the boundary, as a number]

**Desk research** — gather what is already known about the market and the pain
- Without AI: an analyst reads reports, papers and forums — days of work
- With AI (2026): deep research mode plans the search itself, reads hundreds of sources and returns a report with links in 2–30 minutes. Every major assistant has it, free tiers included, with a cap

**The conversation itself**
- Without AI: the researcher runs each interview in person; eight conversations — weeks of calendar
- With AI (2026): the model asks, a live human answers — many times more conversations in the same span

**Synthesising conversations**
- Without AI: transcription by hand, themes picked out by eye
- With AI (2026): transcription is now a free add-on to any call; tools continuously re-cluster themes across the whole accumulated corpus, not one call

[The scale — who is on the other end of the conversation]
human ↔ human — the classic interview · human ↔ model — the machine asks, a live human answers · **model ↔ model — an AI persona: a machine answers too, no live human anywhere in the chain**

Survey of 150 researchers, May 2026: 81% regularly use AI in their work · 8% — as the answering participant · 28% reject that use outright

[Gold callout — what did not change]
A link in a report is not evidence until someone opens it. Measured on 53,090 links from the reports of ten models: **3–13% are fabricated** — those sources never existed. At the lowest rate, 3%, a report with 50 links holds at least one fabricated link with probability **78%**. Deloitte handed exactly such a report to the Australian government for **A$440,000**.

> The share of links that do not open (5–18%) and the caveat about independence of events, which makes 78% an upper bound, were taken off the visible layer after the student review (fix 4, overload) and stay in the notes.

## Speaker notes

What AI changed in this phase — step by step, and at the end the thing it did not change.

Desk research. This used to be days of an analyst's work: reports, papers, forums, complaints. Now deep research mode plans the trajectory of the search itself, reads hundreds of sources and returns a report with links — from two to four minutes up to half an hour. More important than the minutes is something else: by this year every major assistant has that mode, free tiers included. It stopped being an advantage and became the background.

The conversation itself. Here something appeared that did not exist two years ago: the model asks the questions and a live human answers. The researcher writes the guide, the machine then runs conversations in parallel and around the clock, and in the time one in-person interview takes, many times more of them go through. Exactly one side changed — the side that asks.

Synthesising conversations. Transcription has stopped being separate work — it is a free add-on to any call. The difference between tools is now elsewhere: they do not retell one call, they continuously re-cluster themes across the whole accumulated corpus of conversations.

Now about AI personas: they get confused with the previous step constantly. Look at the scale. A human asks a human — the classic. A machine asks a human — the thing that has appeared now. A machine asks a machine — that is an AI persona: there is no live human anywhere in the chain. The profession holds that distinction: in a survey of a hundred and fifty researchers in May this year, eighty-one percent regularly use AI in their work, eight percent use it as the answering participant, and twenty-eight reject that use outright.

And the boundary that has not moved. A link in a report is not evidence until someone opens it. This is measured: across fifty-three thousand links from the reports of ten models and research agents, between three and thirteen percent turned out to be fabricated — they are not even in the web archive, they never existed. The arithmetic from there is plain: at the lowest measured rate, three percent, a report with fifty links holds at least one non-existent link with probability seventy-eight percent. One caveat, which this lecture itself teaches you to demand: the calculation treats the links as independent, and inside one report they are related — the same run, the same model. Seventy-eight is an upper bound. Deloitte handed the Australian government a report for four hundred and forty thousand dollars with exactly that content. It was not the client who found it — it was an outside researcher.
