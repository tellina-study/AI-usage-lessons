## Speaker notes

Computing for AI is starting to physically compete for space not only on Earth. This is a mirror process relative to Section 1: there, the machine moved into space to act where a human cannot be; here, computation moves into space to be where there's more energy.

It's worth saying explicitly what Starcloud and Suncatcher have in common, beyond the bare fact that "this is a computer in space." Both breakdowns share the same type of engineering problem and the same honestly acknowledged limit of applicability: both cases are demonstrators of a new infrastructure category, not a replacement for existing ground-based data centers at scale; both explicitly tie their economic justification to the same launch-cost threshold named in the industry overview; neither has a classical Earth-based equivalent of the task; and both companies openly admit that some engineering decisions can only be verified in actual flight. This isn't a coincidence between two unrelated cases — it's a sign that all of Section 4 describes one and the same new infrastructure category, seen twice, from two different players in the market.
