## Visible slide text

**A field test site, not the Moon.** Lanzarote, Canary Islands — a lava tube chosen specifically for its geological resemblance to lunar skylights.

**In the photo: the Coyote III rover, not a human.** The tether holds the machine, not a climber: the same descent that only a roped human used to make.

## Speaker notes

The photo shows the third phase of COROB-X's field test, shot in March 2023 in Lanzarote by a team from DFKI (Germany) together with Space Applications Services. The Coyote III rover descends on a tether through a skylight opening into a lava tube — a vertical shaft leading into an underground space with no GPS and no daylight. The tether is fed and held by the SherpaTT system, the same hybrid wheel-legged robot that surveys the surface outside and also serves as the anchor for the descent.

Choosing this particular test site wasn't arbitrary: Lanzarote's lava tubes are geologically similar to skylights found on the Moon and on Mars — openings in the surface leading into underground lava tubes, which are being considered as shelter for a future base from radiation and temperature swings. Testing on Earth, under conditions as close to extraterrestrial as possible, is a standard step before a system ever flies.

The shot shows exactly what the COROB-X case study is about: a descent into a tube like this with a tether and safety line used to be done by a human, at risk under an unstable cave ceiling. In this frame, the tether holds the machine — human safety in this specific operation has unambiguously improved, and along with it the complexity of the whole system has grown too: three robots of different designs, a tether, a surface base station, in place of one person with a flashlight.
