## Speaker notes

Five parts — the third application of the lecture's general schema, already familiar from Sections 1 and 2. In this section, the schema unfolds three or four different subject areas at once rather than one — and that is justified because all of them represent one type of AI contribution.

What kind of stage this is. The industry term is AI-assisted design and forecasting: tasks that do not speed up an already-existing process, but make possible something that did not exist at all. What data is obtained. Four different types for four breakdowns: thousands of combustion configuration variants, orbital imagery and terrain, the global state of the atmosphere, the space of possible structural geometries. There is one common question: can you search through, plan, compute, or generate something that previously physically did not fit within an engineer's available time or a computational window. How this data is obtained without a model. Manual engineering calculation and simulation one variant at a time; manual route planning by an engineer from imagery; numerical physical computation on a supercomputer, taking hours across thousands of processors; manual design of a part's geometry in CAD, step by step.

Why does this section come third, after autonomy of action and teleoperation, rather than first? Because intuitively, design and forecasting seem like the most "obvious" niche for artificial intelligence — a task that does not require physical presence anywhere at all. Putting it first would mean starting with the case most intuitively simple and hiding what the section actually shows: even where physical distance does not matter, the model still does not fully replace the classical check. Having gone through Sections 1 and 2, you have already seen this pattern on material where distance was a constraint; here the same pattern is confirmed on material where distance is not a constraint at all — and that is a stronger claim.
