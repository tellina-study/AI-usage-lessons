## Visible slide text

**13 detailed breakdowns + 18 short mentions = 31 AI-in-space cases.** This is the stated denominator: before trusting any percentage or conclusion later in the lecture, it's clear how many cases it's based on.

**Checkability:** each of the 13 breakdowns names a source on its slide — it can be opened and checked against what the lecture says.

## Speaker notes

Before summing up the content, it's worth explicitly naming the base: the lecture's material isn't "a few vivid examples" — it's 13 detailed breakdowns plus 18 more briefly mentioned cases, 31 positions in total, unevenly distributed across the four sections (4, 3, 4, and 2 detailed breakdowns).

Three cases didn't make it into the count of detailed breakdowns, each with an explicitly named reason: the ESA exoskeleton (never flew to space), the DARPA Subterranean Challenge (the lineage of the NeBula/LAMP architecture, not a separate space case in its own right), Pangu-Weather (folded into the AIFS/AIGEFS breakdown as a technical ceiling). Two cases that were detailed breakdowns in an earlier version of the lecture are now reduced to short mentions: NASA IPEx (the only case in the section with no trained model at all) and telesurgery over Starlink (no trained model in the loop). SPOC and predictive latency compensation took their place — both carry a trained model at the very center of the breakdown.

Section 1 holds four detailed breakdowns, not three: LeoLabs answers a direct question left open at the start of the lecture, and carries a task type (prioritization) that none of the section's other three breakdowns show. Section 3 is four, not three, for the same kind of reason: NASA Goddard's generative design carries a qualitatively new type of contribution — the model doesn't predict a number, it produces the geometry itself.
