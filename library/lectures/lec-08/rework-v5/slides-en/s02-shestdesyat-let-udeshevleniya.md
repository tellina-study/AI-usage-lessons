## Visible slide text

**The turning point — the 2000s.** Unit price had already fallen three orders of magnitude here — the market survived, splitting into "cheap and plentiful" and "expensive and exactly this."

**Not one of the seven lines removed the profession that disappeared.**

## Speaker notes

To understand what happened in 2022, you need a scale. The tool of creative production was
getting cheaper long before generative models — getting cheaper regularly and by orders of
magnitude. The seven points on this frame are selected by one criterion: each has a published
number, and each names the operation that disappeared. The operation, specifically — not the
profession — and disappeared, specifically — not "transformed."

The start is 1961, "101 Dalmatians." Before xerography, the ink-and-paint department traced an
animator's pencil drawing by hand onto celluloid: a human worked as a mechanical intermediary
between the idea and the medium. Xerography transferred the drawing directly, and the film's
staff came to about half of "Sleeping Beauty"'s from 1959, at half the cost and half the
schedule. One detail here says more about the nature of such transitions than the numbers
themselves: the story of ninety-nine spotted puppies was chosen precisely because the new method
made it feasible — hand-tracing every spot by hand was simply not realistic. The tool determined
the content. And right there, something moved rightward as a cost: the recognizable scruffy
line of the sixties, the outline printed with all the pencil's roughness, and Walt Disney
publicly disliked that look.

1990, Photoshop 1.0. Before it, professional retouching ran on dedicated Scitex-class systems at
around three hundred dollars an hour; a Photoshop license cost eight hundred ninety-five dollars
as a one-time purchase — paid back in under three hours of Scitex work. The drop in hourly cost
was roughly three orders of magnitude, and with it the prepress shop disappeared as a separate
profession. The retoucher did not go anywhere, though: they moved to a different tool.

Avid is the one point stretched out over time, and that is exactly its value. The first
workstation cost fifty to eighty thousand dollars. By 1992, Avid editing bays stood at major
studios' assistant desks; by 1995, most independent feature films finished on Avid; by 1998, the
Steenbeck flatbed editing table had moved to storage; by 2003, nearly every editing room in North
America had gone nonlinear. Fourteen years from the first installation to full replacement is a
unit for measuring the speed of an industry transition in the pre-digital era, and it is worth
keeping in mind right up to the conversation about music, where the same quantity turns out to
equal eighteen months.

Auto-Tune, 1997, belongs in this row for a lesson that carries over to today without a single
correction. The algorithm grew out of processing seismic data in oil exploration, was applied for
its first year as a targeted patch, and in 1998 Cher's recording "Believe" turned its extreme
settings into an artistic device. A tool made to fix a flaw became the source of a new sound
within a single year — and the argument "this is deceiving the listener" ran word for word the
same as the argument running today about machine vocals.

Microstock is marked as a turning point, and that is not an arbitrary design choice. Classic
agencies sold an image for anywhere from a few hundred to a few thousand dollars; iStock from
2000 and Shutterstock from 2003 started selling for twenty-five cents to a dollar apiece, then by
subscription. The buyer became small business instead of a magazine, the shooter became an
amateur with a now-cheaper digital camera instead of a professional working to commission. In
2006, Getty bought iStock: the owner of an expensive library bought its own price destroyer. This
is the closest historical analog to what is happening today — a three-order-of-magnitude
collapse in unit price in the very same industry, after which the market survived, splitting into
"cheap and plentiful" and "expensive and exactly this." That is why this point is drawn larger
than the rest: we will have to come back to it twice.

Motion capture and StageCraft close out the row. With the first, what moved rightward as a cost
has still not dissolved after more than twenty years: the argument "is this the actor's work or
the animator's work" is not settled, and no Academy acting award has ever gone to a role played
entirely in motion capture. With the second, something different matters: the image here is
already synthetic and at the same time fully deterministic — it was built by hand inside the
engine. The difference from what began in 2022 runs exactly along this line: describing it in
words instead of building it by hand.

Four claims hold across all seven rows at once, and this is the chain's conclusion. The
intermediary operation disappeared, and the profession moved onto a new tool. The volume
produced grew faster than the price fell: after microstock, spending on images stayed roughly the
same, while the number of units bought grew by thousands of times. Every time, an argument about
authorship of the result broke out, and not one of those arguments was ever closed by a technical
counterargument. And the speed of the transition was measured in years — fourteen, for Avid.
These are the four claims against which 2022 should be read.
