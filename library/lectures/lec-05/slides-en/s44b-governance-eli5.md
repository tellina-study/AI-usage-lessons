# Visible content

## Title bar
Governance in plain terms

## Body
**What it is.** An organization-level phase: not "how to build one product," but "what to fund at all" — and stop what doesn't pay off.

**Why.** Resources are limited. The same "continue / kill" gate that applies to a single feature is raised to the level of capital across many initiatives.

**Mental model.** A portfolio funnel: many ideas go in, few funded ones come out. An AI product adds a new variable — the cost of every single request, which has to be weighed against the value of that request.

## Speaker notes

Governance in plain terms is the last, "highest" phase of the loop: we rise from the level of a single product to the level of the whole organization. The question here is not "how do we build this feature," but "what is actually worth funding with money and people, and what should we honestly drop."

The mechanics are the same ones we already saw at launch: the "continue or kill" gate. Except now it allocates not one feature but capital across many initiatives at once. The right image is a portfolio funnel: many ideas go in at the top, each passes through checkpoints, and only a few reach funding. This protects the organization from feeding projects for years that never deliver value.

In this arithmetic, an AI product has a feature of its own — cost per request. Every call to the model costs real money, and that cost has to be weighed against the value the request delivers. Here we will also pull the whole lecture together and answer the question we opened with: why assembly became nearly free, while turning it into value did not. And we'll see how the loud numbers about AI's failures and successes fall apart the moment you ask them four simple questions.
