## Visible slide text

**"Not a restoration in the Scorsese sense" — the project's own authors say so.** No win is declared at all: both classical modes are named, and neither has a price.

## Speaker notes

This is the section's only case with a traceable outcome, and before breaking it down two inconvenient things have to be said about it. First: strictly speaking, this isn't a 3D case. There isn't a single 3D asset or reconstructed scene here — this is upscaling and extending the frame on the sides. It's in this section because the modality has no other case with a traceable outcome, and because it's about the same property — about what counts as already shot. Second: much of what the form of a detailed breakdown requires is missing here, and every gap is named where it belongs, not papered over.

The cost of neither error can be named. Neither the cost of classically reworking an archival film for a non-standard screen, nor the cost of not reworking it — "show it as-is and don't fill the room" — is in the sources. We searched for the project budget or a comparable restoration budget for a feature film; neither the rights holder, nor the technology partner, nor any figures outlet has published one. The only number comes from the outcome side: more than 2.2 million tickets sold from August 2025 through early 2026. The base without which that number doesn't read correctly is a matter of meaning here, not of numbers: tickets are being sold for a screening of a 1939 film available to watch anywhere, for free. People are paying for the one thing that exists nowhere else — the format of the screening.

What the model did is known exactly, even though only its own side is measured: over ninety percent of the film is touched. The 4:3 frame is extended on the sides out to the frame of a giant spherical screen — meaning what Victor Fleming never shot has been made up: continuations of sets, edges of the crowd, patches of sky and road that weren't in the 1939 frame. The project's own authors state this plainly: what they're doing is "not a restoration in the Scorsese sense." What we have is a new work based on the original, and the dispute about that is substantive, not a matter of taste. Restoration recovers what was there and stops where the evidence runs out; here the work continues past that boundary.

The boundary between restoration and invention has moved — and moved silently, with no announcement, inside a piece of work the viewer buys as "that same film." The shift in this boundary isn't measured in money and doesn't show up in any budget; it's visible only when someone asks the question that nobody in this breakdown is in a position to ask. Here people are paying with discernment: a viewer who bought a ticket can't tell Fleming's frame from a frame assembled by the model, just by looking. Worth remembering this case — two sections from now it will pair up with the Herculaneum scroll.

There are two classical modes here, and neither has a number. The first — restoring and reworking for the dome the usual way; the practice exists, its cost isn't in the sources. The second — showing the original 4:3 frame without extension, leaving the rest of the screen dark; the project's own authors name this as the option they considered and rejected, and it has no number either. Since neither classical mode is costed, the breakdown declares no win at all: a win computed from zero measures nothing, and substituting a press-release multiplier for an empty base is off limits.

The mechanism is known by the names of the products used. At the input — the original 1939 film stock, 4:3 frame; at the output — an image for the spherical screen. The extension and infill were done by Imagen, Veo, and Gemini; the upscaling and grain work by Topaz Video AI; Warner Bros. acted as the rights partner. Herbert Stothart's original score was re-recorded, with hall effects added. What the applied models were trained on isn't disclosed. And the breakdown's second significant gap: who made the frame-by-frame decision of "fill in or not," and by what criterion, isn't described in any available source. This is exactly the place where a named person with a named threshold ought to stand, and there isn't one; naming an unnamed performer on the slide would serve no purpose.

Remember this pair of numbers in the right order: over ninety percent of the frame touched — and not one published price for either classical mode. The first number sounds strong and is measured only from one side; the second is silence, and it is exactly the kind of silence that press materials fill with a beautiful multiplier. The breakdown deliberately names no multiplier here, and that's a decision, not a gap in preparation: a win computed from zero measures nothing but the willingness of whoever did the counting.

I'll repeat the formulation, because it's the load-bearing engineering move of the whole evening. To name a win, you need both sides in one unit: what the work cost by the classical mode and what it costs with the model. Here only the second half exists — and that second half is measured, on top of everything, not in money but in the share of the frame touched, that is, in an entirely different unit than the one in which the classical rework would have been costed. Two different units placed side by side don't make a comparison; they make an impression.

The most painful thing in this breakdown isn't the money — that may genuinely be commercial secrecy here. The hole is in what isn't described: who, and by what threshold, made the frame-by-frame decision of "fill in or not." That is exactly the place where the boundary between restoration and invention runs, and that decision was made by a living person — otherwise the frame wouldn't exist. In any breakdown you're shown, look for the place where a decision is named and the person who made it. A named technology doesn't substitute for that place. Where there's a decision but no one who made it, you're being shown a product description.

For practice, this leads to a short check. On hearing a claim of the form "the system did X," ask three questions in order: in what unit is X measured; is what came before the system measured in that same unit; and is it named who, inside the process, made the decisions that can't be automated. If there's an answer to the first question and no answer to the second and third — you're looking at a press release, even if it's published in a scientific format.

And one more detail that matters for reading any such breakdown. The names of the products used here are known — Imagen, Veo, Gemini for the infill, Topaz Video AI for upscaling and grain — but what those models were trained on isn't disclosed. Knowing a product's name and knowing what's inside it are different things, and the first often stands in for the second. Judging the result requires the second: it's the training material that determines which continuations of a set the model finds plausible, and that's exactly what stays behind a closed door.

The last thing worth taking from this slide. The Wizard of Oz case isn't about whether this is good or bad. It's about the fact that a decision about the boundary between restoration and invention was made inside the production process, with no announcement, and the viewer buying a ticket doesn't know that decision was made. Two sections from now this case will pair up with the Herculaneum scrolls, where the technique is neighboring — a trained model restores an image that isn't in the source data — but the ethics are the opposite, and exactly one feature tells them apart: whether there's an independent way to verify the result.
