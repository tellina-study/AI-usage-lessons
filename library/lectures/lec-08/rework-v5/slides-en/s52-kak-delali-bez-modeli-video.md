## Visible slide text


## Speaker notes

Without this part, any gain would have to be counted from zero, and the course doesn't accept a gain counted from zero. There are two units of account here at once, and keeping them apart is mandatory: the cost of a finished episode makes sense for a series, the cost of an ad for advertising, and folding them into one table would misrepresent what's being compared. The cost of one episode of a 2024 Chinese micro-drama is given on the slide in yuan: over one million, which at the time came to about 137 thousand dollars.

A film crew's shift is the basic unit of classical production. For an ad of technical complexity comparable to the section's generative cases — a crew of fifty-plus and a production cycle of a year. The Russian baseline band for the same craft is published in industry price surveys: a basic TV spot runs 300,000–800,000 rubles with one to two days of studio shooting and a crew of 3–5; a mid-tier one, with actors and two to four shooting days, runs 800,000–2,500,000 rubles; a premium one, with a crew of 10–20 and special effects, runs 2,500,000–10,000,000 rubles and up. A well-known actor's fee runs from 500,000 rubles per shooting day. Music is counted separately: a licensed library track is 10,000–50,000 rubles, an original score from 150,000 rubles.

The Irishman, 2019, is the control point for "expensive" right before the generative era. The budget grew from a stated $125 million to $159 million, and the growth is attributed specifically to the digital de-aging of three actors: about 1,750 shots, the largest use of the technique in a feature film at the time. Preparation at the effects studio started in 2015, four years before release. The effects supervisor made a point of saying that, per shot, it costs the same as in an ordinary effects-heavy film, and it comes out expensive because there are enough shots here for two films. This caveat has to be kept: it is the correct unit of account, and without it the $159 million reads as the price of the technology, when it is really the price of volume.

Non-linear editing, 1989. Before it, editing meant physically splicing film on an editing table. A single workstation cost $50,000–80,000. The replacement timeline that follows is worth keeping whole: 1992 — editing stations appear for assistants at major studios; 1995 — most independent features are finished on them; 1998 — the editing table moves into storage; 2003 — almost all of North America's editing rooms are non-linear. Fourteen years from the first workstation to full replacement. This is the unit for measuring the speed of an industry transition in the pre-digital era, and later in the section it serves as a baseline: the Chinese micro-drama will need about a year for the same path.

Toy Story, 1995, is the benchmark for what used to be called "impossibly expensive." A $30 million budget against $373 million in worldwide box office. The render farm: 87 dual-processor and 30 quad-processor workstations at 100 megahertz. One frame rendered in anywhere from 45 minutes to 30 hours depending on complexity; a frame took 300 megabytes of memory. The whole film — about 800,000 machine-hours for 114,200 frames, that is, for 79 minutes of screen time. It's against this figure that today's "ten times faster" and "a shot in seconds" should be read: thirty years ago a synthetic frame cost machine-hours; now it costs cents.

StageCraft, October 2019, is the real-time classic. The first permanent virtual-production stage: 1,326 LED panels at a 2.84-millimeter pitch, 6 meters tall, a 270-degree arc, a stage 23 meters across; the image is computed by a real-time engine and goes straight into the camera. More than half of the first season was shot inside this stage. An important detail for the lecture's axis: this is already image generation without shooting — just deterministic. The picture here was synthetic too; the difference from 2022–2026 is that back then it had to be built by hand in the engine, and now it can be described in words.

The five points above add up to a single chain, and its meaning is the same as the chain's in the still-image section. Each time, an intermediary operation disappeared: physically splicing film, going out for a background, tracing an outline frame by frame. Each time, the profession moved onto a new tool, the volume produced grew faster than the unit price fell, and each time an argument broke out about authorship of the result — the argument over performance capture has run since 2002 and isn't settled yet: no Academy acting award has ever gone to a role performed entirely in motion capture. This is exactly the same argument now running around machine voice editing and machine shot generation.

All five classical modes — a camera crew's shift, graphics from a contractor, non-linear editing, frame-by-frame computation on a render farm, a real-time LED stage — share a property worth stating on its own: in none of them is a failed attempt free. A shooting day costs a shooting day, a machine-hour costs a machine-hour, a panel costs a panel. It is exactly this property that stopped holding in 2022, and the three breakdowns that follow count by how much.
