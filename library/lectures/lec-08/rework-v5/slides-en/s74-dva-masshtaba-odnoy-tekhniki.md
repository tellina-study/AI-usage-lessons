## Visible slide text

**"Can the model do it" is almost always "yes" — and that settles nothing.** What settles it is cost, frequency per second, and whose hardware.

## Speaker notes

If there's one thought to carry out of this whole section's design, it's this one. Both figures above are measured. A little over five quadrillion operations per frame, over half a minute — that's how a radiance field was computed in 2020. One millisecond per frame, sixty times a second, five neural networks per frame — that's how neural graphics is computed on a gaming computer in 2025.

There's no different science and no different class of model between these two regimes. Architecture is heading toward a transformer in both cases: the radiance field got there via diffusion straight in 3D, real-time graphics got there by swapping a convolutional model for a transformer one, and in the same month, January 2025, no less. Training in both cases is by gradient descent. The difference is created by the compute budget per unit of output, and it differs by roughly thirty thousand times.

The milliseconds here are the load-bearing numbers, and they're given by the manufacturer as a direct quote. In the previous generation, one 4K frame was generated in about three and a quarter milliseconds on the flagship card of the time. In the new one, each of the three new frames per rendered frame is generated in about one millisecond on average, on the flagship card of today. The technique behind this is worth naming: the neural portion is cut in half — the larger half is computed once per pair of input frames and reused, the smaller half is computed for every synthesized frame. The hardware optical-flow accelerator, which in the previous generation occupied a separate block of silicon, has been replaced by a neural network. Five networks run per frame, and all of it fits inside a few milliseconds — otherwise, as the manufacturer itself says, the technology would have become a slowdown.

The network solves two different problems. The first is upscaling: the engine renders the frame at low resolution, and the network reconstructs the high-resolution one, drawing on the history of previous frames plus motion vectors and the depth buffer that the engine itself hands it. The second is frame generation: the model synthesizes an intermediate frame between two rendered ones. The generation released in January 2025 brought an architecture change directly parallel to what was happening in image generation: the convolutional model was replaced by a transformer one.

The mandatory second half — what this doesn't do, and the "what it can't do" list is half the content here, because every case follows from the method's design, not from some unfinished version. Frame generation doesn't reduce input latency: the intermediate frame is shown between two already-finished frames, the player sees more frames, but the reaction to their action isn't sped up by a single millisecond — the frame rate went up, responsiveness didn't, and that's exactly the case where the metric improved rather than the thing the metric was introduced to measure. The manufacturer names the hard spots itself: the UI, which has no motion vectors — there's simply nowhere to get them from, because the UI isn't part of the 3D scene; glints and reflections, for which motion vectors are wrong by construction, because a reflection doesn't move the way the reflecting surface moves; particles, rays, and translucency, for which the depth buffer is wrong — a translucent pixel doesn't have one depth. Their own wording is precise: such effects "tend to dominate user perception even though their share of pixels is relatively small" — the eye latches onto exactly these, and the artifact is more noticeable there than on any wall. Separately named is ghosting trailing behind moving objects — a defect of the convolutional model, and the very thing the transformer was put in to fix.

For an engineer this leads to a working rule, and it's broader than graphics. When discussing applying a model in a product, the discussion is almost always about budget. The question "can the model do this" is answered "yes" most of the time today, and that answer settles nothing; what settles it is three other things — at what cost, how many times per second, and on whose hardware. Neural real-time graphics exists because someone found a way to fit it inside sixteen milliseconds. If a system improves a metric indirectly — by filling in, predicting, interpolating — ask which properties of the original process survived and which didn't: usually what survives is what the metric measures, and what's lost is what the metric was introduced for in the first place.

Positions in this market shift quarterly, and the numbers above should be checked on the day of reading. In the year following the generation described, two separate events happened that are easy to merge into one: in January 2026 the second generation of the transformer upscaling model shipped, and on March 31, 2026, a dynamic frame multiplier with a sixfold multiplication mode shipped. At the competing manufacturer, the first machine-learning-based solution shipped in March 2025: a hybrid of a convolutional and a transformer network, also in eight-bit floating point. The same class of task for film and archives is solved by frame interpolation: the RIFE method claims a speedup of four to twenty-seven times against the predecessors it names, at thirty-odd frames per second for 2x interpolation of 720p on a 2018 consumer card.

The other end of the same scale is the direction called world models. A language model predicts the next token conditioned on the previous ones, a video model the next frame conditioned on the previous ones and a description, a world model the next frame conditioned on the previous ones, an internal representation of the scene, and the user's action. The added term changes the genre: the output stops being a recording and becomes an environment. There are two measurable figures for this direction so far, and both were named earlier. There's already a case of industrial use for the direction: since February 2026, Waymo has used a model based on Genie 3 to simulate rare driving scenarios when training drivers. What it doesn't have has to be said just as plainly: a costed outcome (a comparison with real safety figures), information on inference cost, and a comparison with the classical mode — building an interactive environment in an engine — in either time or money.

The share of the screen drawn by the network — fifteen out of every sixteen pixels at 4x frame generation together with 4x upscaling. This number is worth saying out loud at least once, because it overturns the usual split between "real graphics" and "AI pictures." Someone who counts a browser image generator as artificial intelligence and doesn't count the quality setting in their own game is wrong, exactly backwards: the second one is running on their own desk sixty times a second and drawing almost the entire screen.
