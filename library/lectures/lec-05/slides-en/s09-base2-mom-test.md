# Visible content

## Title bar
Even your mother will lie — if the question is built wrong

## Body
[3 rule cards in a row]

**The Mom Test — 3 rules**
1. Talk about their life, not your idea
2. Ask about the past, not a hypothetical future
3. Validate with a commitment, not a compliment

[Contrast, Ocean rounded box]
Bad: "Would you pay $20 a month?" → Good: "How much do you pay today for the closest alternative?"

## Speaker notes

The Mom Test is a single-step technique: how to correctly run one conversation with one person so you don't fool yourself. The method was formulated by Rob Fitzpatrick. The name reflects a provocative premise: even your own mother will lie to you about the quality of your business idea if you ask her directly — and the defect isn't in the respondent's honesty, it's in the construction of the question.

Three rules, concrete and directly applicable. First: talk about their life, not your idea — never pitch the idea and then ask for a verdict. Second: ask about specifics from the past, not opinions about the future — hypothetical questions provoke dishonest optimism. Third: talk less, listen more, and validate only with a real commitment, never a compliment — legitimate signals are time, reputation, money.

A good illustration: instead of "would you pay twenty dollars a month" — "how much do you pay today for the closest alternative to this task." The second question anchors on real past behavior and money the respondent is already spending. A limitation of the method: it's an interview technique, not a sampling or synthesis method — it doesn't tell you how many people to interview. It's also useful here to fix the divide between qualitative and quantitative research: qualitative answers "why" on a small sample and generates hypotheses; quantitative answers "how much" at scale and tests a hypothesis that already exists. This isn't a hierarchy but a division of labor — and we'll need it again in section four.
