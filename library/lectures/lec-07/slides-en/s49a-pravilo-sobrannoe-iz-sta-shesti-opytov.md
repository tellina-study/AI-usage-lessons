## Visible slide text

**A preregistered review with a meta-analysis.** Only studies measuring all three quantities were included: the human alone, the model alone, and the pair.

**The headline figure for the whole review is minus 0.23:** on average, the pair is worse than the stronger of the two sides. From this figure, both halves of the review read as parts, not as the final verdict.

**Where the model is stronger than the human, both figures are measured on the exact same studies:** the pair is worse than the model by 0.54, while also beating the human alone by 0.74. Both together are the rule: the pair outperforms the weaker side and doesn't catch up with the stronger one.

*Across the whole review, the pair beats the human alone by 0.64 — but that's a different base and different studies, and this number doesn't go next to minus 0.54.*

## Speaker notes

This section's rule is built from a review of a hundred six comparisons. The three case studies before it gave three different outcomes: a drop in sensitivity in the eye-tracking study, both signs at once in pathology, and a gain on the control line. A single trial, however vividly measured, remains a single case, and three single cases don't add up to a rule either. To talk about a rule, you need a review, and one exists: a preregistered review with a meta-analysis, 74 studies out of 5,126 screened, 106 unique experiments, 370 effect sizes, publications from January 2020 through June 2023. The inclusion criterion decides everything here: a study was only included if all three quantities were measured — the human alone, the model alone, and the pair. Without the third quantity there's nothing to compare the pair against, and that's exactly why most deployment reports never answer this question.

Effect size is a measure of "how far it shifted," expressed in units of the data's own spread. It's needed where you have to combine a hundred six studies on different scales: one measured the share of correct answers, another an expert score, a third, time. Numbers like this are read by two features: the sign, and whether the interval sits entirely on one side of zero. An interval from −0.71 to −0.37 is negative throughout — meaning the effect will stay negative if the studies are repeated. A third decimal digit carries no real meaning here: in substance it's "about half a spread's worth downward" against "about half a spread's worth upward."

The caveat travels with the figure every time. The review's headline figure of −0.23, with an interval from −0.39 to −0.07, is computed against the better of the two sides; the human isn't the baseline here. It's precisely against this figure that minus 0.54 reads as a subset of the studies, not as the final verdict.

Next comes a distinction that's easy to slip on. The study has TWO "pair versus human alone" figures, and they're computed on different bases. The first — across the whole review: 0.64, with an interval from 0.53 to 0.74, a statistic with ninety-eight degrees of freedom. The second — on the same subset of studies where the model is stronger than the human: 0.74, with an interval from 0.63 to 0.85, a hundred four degrees of freedom, meaning exactly the same studies used to compute minus 0.54. This slide's point — "the pair beats the human and falls short of the stronger side" — is stated with the SECOND pair, minus 0.54 and 0.74, because both halves of it stand on the same subset of studies and on comparable degrees of freedom. Putting 0.64 next to minus 0.54 would collapse two different questions on two different subsets of studies into one conclusion, and the room would read them as two halves of the same measurement.

The opposite subset is named too: where the human is stronger, the pair beats both sides — 0.46, with an interval from 0.28 to 0.66. The reading "the human-and-model pair works poorly" isn't supported by this data. There's one correct formulation: the pair is usually better than the human and worse than whichever of the two is stronger.

What the review doesn't say. It isn't about patient outcomes, heterogeneity within it is high, and medical studies make up only part of it. No form from the vocabulary applies here: behind this review there's no deployed process at all — no location, no workload, no performer — so there's nothing to fit to the nearest form. The rule that follows is short: first measure who's stronger on their own, then decide who decides.

**Sources.** [Vaccaro, Almaatouq, Malone, Nature Human Behaviour 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1) · [a preprint of the same study, open access](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06087)

What of this gets said aloud, while being removed from the slide itself. First: a rule isn't built from a single trial — here it is, assembled from a hundred six studies by a preregistered meta-analysis, and that's a different strength of evidence than any of the four case studies before it. Second: after this slide, the question "does AI help" reads differently — as a question about who in this pair is stronger on this particular task, and that question is checkable, unlike the original one. Third: the rule isn't tied to a modality — it's assembled from studies with text, images, and tabular data all at once, and that's exactly why it can be carried out of the lecture and applied to your own work.

And one last thing, without which this slide reads as a verdict against the pair. The pair doesn't inherit the strength of the stronger side — but it does beat the human alone, and beats it robustly: 0.74 on the same subset of studies, with an interval from 0.63 to 0.85; 0.64 across the whole review, with an interval from 0.53 to 0.74 — both entirely above zero. So the question isn't whether to bring the model into the work, but which side you measure the gain against. Comparing against a human without the model gives one picture; comparing against whichever side is stronger gives another, and almost any deployment report knows how to swap the second for the first.
