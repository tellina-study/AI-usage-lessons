## Speaker notes

Section 3 — Design and new capabilities. Surrogate ML models and generative structural design aren't a space-industry invention: they're general engineering machine-learning techniques, used in the auto industry too. But the specific application — route planning for a rover by a general-purpose model with a subsequent digital-twin check, and designing real structures for the future flagship telescope via generative search — was physically impossible before sufficiently powerful general-purpose models appeared, and before there was willingness to trust them with a draft version. This is carrying a ready-made tool into a new role, not inventing an algorithm from scratch.

Section 4 — Space for AI. The idea of putting computing where there's more energy isn't new in itself — that's how data centers end up next to dams on Earth. But the engineering of an orbital data center — radiation hardening, heat rejection in vacuum, servicing with no human access — didn't exist before this specific project: these problems hadn't come up in any Earth-based data center configuration.

Overall count: of the four sections, two (Section 1 and Section 4) have a measured case of a solution born specifically from the frontier's pressure; in the other two (Section 2 and Section 3), space mostly carries a ready-made tool into a new, previously impossible configuration. This is the honest answer to the lecture's question: not "space always invents" and not "space only applies" — it depends. This 2:2 split wasn't a target set in advance when the material was prepared — it turned up as a consequence of honestly applying the three selection conditions to the material the research turned up. It would have been tempting to build the lecture so that all four sections demonstrated the same thesis — material could have been found for that, if we'd wanted to select only the cases that fit. We deliberately don't do that.
