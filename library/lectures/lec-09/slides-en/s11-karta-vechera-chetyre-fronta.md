## Speaker notes

Section 1 is this lecture's maximum autonomy: later on we'll show a lesser degree of autonomy (Section 2) or shift sideways to other kinds of task (Sections 3–4). Section 2 is the minimum autonomy: a human still holds control, just at a distance, and it comes right after Section 1 on purpose, so that the boundary of "how far can a human still control this" is visible against the maximum we just showed. Section 3 isn't about autonomy at all, but about a new kind of task: design and forecasting that used to require either a human at a drafting table or a supercomputer. Section 4 is a mirror of the whole lecture: it's not space getting smarter with AI, it's AI starting to physically change where compute infrastructure is placed.

Looking at this sequence not as four separate blocks but as one path, you can see the question itself gradually shift. Section 1 asks: can the machine decide on its own? — and answers "yes, where the task is geometric or admits an easily trainable model". Section 2 asks: where's the boundary past which a human can no longer decide alone? — and answers "where communication delay or the speed of events physically leaves no time". Section 3 doesn't ask about autonomy of action, but about autonomy of thinking: can a model design or forecast something that used to require only a human? — and answers "yes, but the final check stays classical". Section 4 no longer asks about the task itself, but about its physical location: where is it cheaper to compute? — and answers "mostly on Earth for now, but the threshold for a different answer has already been named as a number". Four questions, not one — and that's exactly why the lecture is split into four sections rather than one general case study of "AI in space". After the four sections, the Wrap-up will bring the thirteen case studies together into a single answer to the lecture's question and a shared tally, and the Closing will name the debt carried forward.
