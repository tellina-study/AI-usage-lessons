## Visible slide text

# The frontier that becomes its own laboratory

### AI in civilian space

Lecture 9

The solutions space forced us to invent — and that did not exist before it.

## Speaker notes

The image on this slide is not mood-setting decoration. It is the entrance to the Marius Hills lunar lava tube, photographed by the orbiting LRO spacecraft: a real opening in the Moon's surface leading into an empty underground space with no GPS, no daylight, and no connection to the surface. This is exactly where, in this very tube, this lecture's reconnaissance team of three robots will arrive — and the exact device that leads them there was born not in space, but at the bottom of an earthly mine, in a DARPA competition for underground reconnaissance. That is the whole lecture's claim, in one frame, before it starts proving it piece by piece: the familiar answer is "engineering solutions come from the lab, and space just applies them" — and today we will test that answer with thirteen concrete case studies.

The lecture is about civilian space: Mars rovers, orbital dockings, weather forecasting, computation in orbit. In an hour and a half we will go through four types of AI contribution — not organized by geography (not "the Moon", not "Mars", not "orbit"), but by what exactly AI does: decides and acts on its own, controls at a distance, designs what didn't exist before, and finally, quite literally, moves into space computationally, because there's more sun there.

The lecture's throughline question is this: which decisions did space force us to invent — and did they exist before it demanded them? The answer will be given right at the end, named case by case for each section, not as one general phrase right now.
