## Visible slide text

**Section 2. Speech Synthesis, Performer Voice, and Music**

Sound is the only modality where the section's main claim is checked by the listener's own ear in ten seconds, with no table at all. That is why here the demonstration comes first, the description after.

**Three breakdowns, in order:** a voice for someone whose disease was taking it away · dubbing and localization · Val Kilmer's voice, given back.

**Six mentions, each with a number. The live demonstration plays on the room's speakers.**

## Speaker notes

The second section is built differently from the others, and that is deliberate. Every other section opens with a description of the stage and only then arrives at examples; here the order is reversed. The reason sits in the modality itself. Sound is the only one of the five where the main claim can be checked by the listener's ear in ten seconds and without a single figure: first the original recording of a human voice plays, then, right after it, the synthesized copy of that same voice. A still image cannot be presented this way — it is studied at leisure; a moving image needs a minute of screen time; a 3D scene needs interactivity the room does not have. Ten seconds of sound is a cheaper and more convincing demonstration than any other in the lecture.

The second reason is rhythmic. A form repeated six times in a row stops working: the listener starts predicting the next screen and stops following the content. One deliberate break in rhythm was planned in advance, and it was placed here because here the break has a substantive justification, not just a formal one.

Under the word "sound," five different crafts of the industry live, and from here on they are kept apart by name: speech synthesis, voice cloning, localization and dubbing, music generation, and source separation. The last one stands apart — by the very framing of the task it is not generation at all, and it is exactly on this craft that the section shows the boundary between restoring what was recorded and inventing what never existed.

The section's three detailed breakdowns are chosen to show three different states of the count. In the first, the second classical mode is named and has no number of its own — that is said out loud. In the second, both sides are counted in the same unit, and the gap between them is measured in the thousands. In the third, a classical mode does not exist at all, and there is a medical reason for it. Three different situations in which an engineer has to make a decision, and all three come up in practice more often than one would like.

Six mentions follow, each with a number and a basis. A mention with no number is not placed in this section at all: a name without an observation is a bibliography entry, and its place is in the reading list.
