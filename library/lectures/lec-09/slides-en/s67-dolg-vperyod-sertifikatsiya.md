## Visible slide text

**Every organization has its own strict internal approval procedure.** But a common, cross-agency, international standard specifically for autonomous behavior — unlike the certification standards for the aircraft or spacecraft itself — **doesn't exist.**

**This isn't specific to space** — it's a general feature of every technology that develops faster than the institutional structure meant to regulate it.

## Speaker notes

This lecture, like most of the course's industry lectures, has a named gap that isn't closed by this lecture's material and carries forward through the course: certifying autonomous systems for real industrial deployment. Not one of the thirteen breakdowns flies or operates in a mode where a regulator has formally and in advance certified the system's autonomous behavior as such — the component gets certified, the campaign gets tested, the demonstrator gets approved, but a formal body of requirements for autonomous AI decisions inside space systems, comparable in rigor to the requirements for the rocket itself, doesn't exist in any of the jurisdictions discussed here.

Dragon's manual intervention is an engineering decision by the company itself, built into the design, not a requirement from an outside regulator. NASA IPEx's campaign-level supervised autonomy is an architectural decision by its developers. AIFS and AIGEFS went into operation through ECMWF's and NOAA's own procedures respectively — an internal process of a specific agency, not a general international standard. The difference between "the developer has its own strict procedure" and "a common regulatory standard exists for the whole technology category" is exactly the gap that automotive or aviation certification standards close in other industries, and this one doesn't, not yet.

This doesn't mean the industry operates with no oversight at all — every system discussed here has its own, often quite strict, engineering approval procedure within its own organization. This is a general feature of every technology that develops faster than the institutional structure regulating it can keep up — space is no exception here, it's one example of a general rule.
