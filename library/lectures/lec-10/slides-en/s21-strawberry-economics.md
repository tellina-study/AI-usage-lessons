## Speaker notes

The third second-level failure — more precisely, a drawn-out pilot phase that doesn't turn into production — is the strawberry-picking robot. Dozens of startups in California, the UK, and Japan have been working on this for ten to fifteen years already.

The 2024 economics. One strawberry-picking robot costs from two hundred to three hundred fifty thousand dollars of capex. Annualized capex plus maintenance — from sixty-eight to one hundred thirty thousand dollars per year. The addressable manual-labor market for strawberry picking in the US is about fifty billion dollars. Robots occupy less than five percent of this market. In California, picking-labor costs come to forty-three thousand dollars per acre per year for harvesting alone.

The structural cause of the stall. Strawberry picking is a task in which the human baseline "learns in days". An experienced picker distinguishes ripe from unripe berries, doesn't break the fruit, avoids broken shoots, works at different speeds depending on the density of the crop. The robotics-ML stack doesn't catch up to this baseline over years and tens of millions of dollars of R&D. The quote that has become canonical in the literature: "harvesting is the last great unsolved problem in agricultural robotics".

The alternative: H-2A guest worker programs — agricultural-worker visas in the US — plus ergonomic improvements. Seated harvesters, partially mechanized platforms on which workers sit and pick with one hand — reduce physical strain without trying to fully automate the task. This is still the right path — until robotics catches up.

Tevel Aerobotics from the previous slide is an example of a specialized niche where the task is simpler: apples hang on the tree, they're easier to grasp than low-growing strawberries. This illustrates the same specialization principle: a narrow niche works, generic doesn't.

DailyRobotics announced a commercial launch of a robotic strawberry picker in California for 2026. This is one more promise that needs to be verified. The category pattern — dozens of startups in the pilot phase, a handful with commercial revenue, and for every year of delay more startups are added, promising a "breakthrough in eighteen months". An engineer should treat such promises with the same skepticism as an "AI agronomic advisor in a year".
