# Visible content

## Title bar
An LLM is good exactly where the task is text-and-dialogue — and bad where it is not.

## Body
[Left — Ocean rounded box]

**Where an LLM is the right type in a bank:**
- client support — answers to routine questions
- voice assistant — recognize the request, walk through the script
- assistance to an employee — summarize an inquiry, suggest the regulation
- explaining a product in plain language

*This is exactly what an LLM was designed for (unlike Sections 1–3).*

**Scale (verified):**
- the **T-Bank** chatbot handles over **40%** of client inquiries *(TAdviser review, 2025)*
- around **70% of banks** planned voice assistants by 2025

[Right — Ocean rounded box, fact integrity = the lesson of the lecture itself]

**Why NOT «>90% of banks' inquiries»**
The ~90% figure is real — but it is the share of calls handled by a voice assistant **in one bank's call center**, not the share of banks' inquiries overall. This is **base substitution** (class 5 of fact-checking).

[Gold callout, bottom]
The lecture teaches fact-checking — building an assertion on a figure with a substituted base would violate the very principle it teaches. A precise modest figure is more valuable than a loud one with a substituted base.

## Speaker notes

A task where a language model really fits in a bank: client support with answers to routine questions, a voice assistant that recognizes a request and walks through the script, assistance to an employee in the form of summarizing an inquiry and suggesting the regulation, explaining a product in plain language. These are text-and-dialogue tasks — exactly what a language model was designed for, unlike forecasting, anomalies, and scoring. Scale: the client's first contact with the bank is increasingly automated. Per verified data, the T-Bank chatbot handles over forty percent of client inquiries, and around seventy percent of banks planned to launch voice assistants by 2025.

Here is an important caveat about reliability, and it is also an illustration of the lecture's theme. The course's normative materials contain the wording «voice assistants handle more than ninety percent of banks' inquiries». The figure of around ninety percent exists somewhere — but it refers to the share of calls handled by a voice assistant in one bank's call center, not to the share of banks' inquiries overall. This is a classic base substitution: a real metric about calls in one bank's call center is expanded to an incorrect industry-wide wording. Therefore in the lecture we use only the verified wording with attribution and a date, not an unconfirmed figure. This is not pedantry: the lecture teaches fact-checking — building an assertion on a figure with a substituted base would violate the very principle it teaches.

And let us underscore the symmetry. In forecasting, anomalies, and scoring, a language model is the wrong type, because the tasks are not textual. In support and explanation it is the right type, because the task is precisely text-and-dialogue. The lecture's thesis is symmetric: «a different task — a different type of AI» means not «the LLM is bad» but «the LLM is good exactly where the task is text-and-dialogue, and bad where it is not».
