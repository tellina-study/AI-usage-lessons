# Incident 2. New York City's Municipal Chatbot

## Assertion

The answers sounded authoritative and rested on material that was genuinely on-topic for the question — but interpreting that material led to a conclusion that was wrong, and in places unlawful.

## Visual

> A separate incident from 2024, documented by journalistic investigation: New York City's municipal chatbot, built to help small-business owners, several times gave direct questions about city rules confident, grammatically flawless answers that were legally incorrect or directly contradicted city law — for example, the bot said employers could keep employees' tips for themselves, and that landlords could refuse tenants with housing subsidies.

> Both incidents technically worked correctly: they found text relevant to the topic and built a grammatically correct answer on it. The failure sits in the assumption that topical closeness equals factual correctness — the search mechanics have nothing to do with it.

## Speaker notes

A separate incident from that same year, 2024, documented by journalistic investigation (The Markup, Colin Lecher, March 29) — New York City's municipal chatbot, MyCity, built to help small-business owners. On direct questions about city rules, it several times gave confident, grammatically flawless answers that turned out to be legally incorrect or to directly contradict city law — for example, the bot said an employer could keep employees' tips for themselves, and that landlords could refuse tenants with housing subsidies. The answers sounded authoritative and were built on material that was genuinely on-topic for the question — but the selection and interpretation of that material led to a wrong conclusion, delivered with exactly the same confidence as a correct one.

A broken tool has nothing to do with it — not in the pizza incident, not in the business chatbot incident. Technically, both worked correctly: they found text relevant to the topic and built a grammatically correct, coherent answer on it. The failure sits in the assumption that topical closeness equals factual correctness — the search mechanics have nothing to do with it. This is the same lesson we already saw in this session's fourth case — "answers without an error" doesn't mean "uses the input correctly" — only one level further up: "found a relevant chunk" doesn't mean "gave a correct answer."

It's worth separately noting how this incident differs from the previous one, and why both are needed together. The pizza incident was about a source that, in form, was a harmless joke; it was never meant as a serious recommendation — it's easy to imagine a filter that would screen out sources that are obviously not serious. The chatbot incident is harder: the material it relied on was real, official, and on-topic — the problem is how a wrong conclusion was assembled out of material that was correct in form. Together, these two incidents show that the danger isn't limited to one type of bad source — it's broader than that, reaching into the very idea that "found something relevant" means "answered correctly."
