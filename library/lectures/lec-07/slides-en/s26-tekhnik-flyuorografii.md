## Visible slide text

The classical regime of this stage — **quality control of the measurement.**

**The rule the technician decides by:** the room's own instructions and the visible quality of the frame. The device carries no such rule.

**What happens if an ungradable image goes to reading anyway:** the finding gets made on what is visible — and down the chain follow a wrong diagnosis, a repeat study, lost weeks, and treatment started too late. **Nobody notices,** until a review is needed.

**With the model, the fork does not disappear — it splits in two:** it rejects an ungradable image itself — and then the person gets a repeat visit; if it does not reject it, it reads whatever it was given.

## Speaker notes

The classical regime of this stage is quality control of the measurement, and it is decided by the technician at the machine. He has two rules: the room's own instructions, and the visible quality of the frame. The device itself carries no such rule, and the technician's decision rarely makes it into any document. This is work done entirely without a model, so this slide does not deal in numbers for the cost of either error or a count against the model — and it should not.

A chest-X-ray room in a district clinic. The technician positions the patient at the machine, takes the image, looks at the screen, and decides: usable, or retake. He decides based on what is visible in the frame — patient positioning, field coverage, the presence of artifacts, visibility of what is supposed to be visible. This rule lives in the room's own instructions and in the eyes of whoever took the image; it does not exist in the device at all. The diagram on the slide is exactly these three steps, and there is nothing more to the classical regime than that.

This work has its own unit of count, and it needs naming right here, while the regime is still classical: one gradability decision per image, made on the spot and almost never recorded. No record is left — which means there is no way, from the documents, to later count how many images got retaken. That is exactly why, in the breakdown that follows, the share of ungradable images came as a surprise to everyone: in the classical order nobody measured it, because there was nothing to measure it from.

If an ungradable image goes to reading, what gets read is an artifact — and the reader may never learn this, because he never saw the patient. The diagnosis gets made on what is visible, not on what was actually there. There is nobody but the technician to catch the substitution: no feedback ever comes back to him, and until a case gets reviewed, nobody usually finds out the image was ungradable at all.

Hence a general statement worth making before any breakdown at all: before any reading happens, someone decides whether the measurement is fit for use, and that someone is a human. Quality control is a procedure, and it never becomes a property of the device.

This work has a volume, and that volume is measured. The technician retakes the image, the patient changes a second time, a line forms. In the breakdown that follows, this work is counted directly: in autonomous reading of fundus images across eleven clinics, 393 of 1,838 images were rejected for quality — that is, 21 out of every hundred, over the first six months.

And here is where the scene stops being mundane. As long as a human does the reading, gradability stays an internal matter of the room: not satisfied — retake. When the reading becomes autonomous and happens on the spot, gradability turns into a condition the result depends on: there is nobody to order a retake, and nobody for a specialist to check the finding against either. Of the three reading regimes — double reading, arbitration, and quality control — this section breaks down only the third; the first two come later, together with the human who reads alongside the model, and are counted there.

It is worth saying why this slide is its own slide at all, rather than a line in the stage's description. Of the three classical reading regimes — double reading, arbitration, quality control — this section breaks down one, and it is this one because it is exactly the regime the very first breakdown will catch already changed. Double reading and arbitration arrive in the next section, together with the human who reads alongside the model, and are counted there in numbers. Here they are only named.

A question for the room fits here — a prediction on this slide's fork. With the model, the decision on image gradability splits in two: it rejects an ungradable image itself, and then the person gets a repeat visit; if it does not reject it, it reads whatever it was given. I ask: which of this fork's two errors costs the system more — rejecting a usable image, or reading an ungradable one? And I ask for one phrase of justification. There is no "universal" right answer: it depends on who pays for the repeat visit and who is held responsible for a missed disease — and that is the entire point of the question. The different answers in the room are exactly the material for the next step: the cost of an error is set before any accuracy figure, and it is not the model that sets it.
