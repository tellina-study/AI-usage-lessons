## Visible slide text

# 1. Primary screening and selection for examination

**Who to look at in the first place.** Screening is examining people with no complaints, by a formal criterion. Before anyone gets treated, someone decides who's even worth looking at.

In order:

**Example 1, without a model: a Swedish program calls people in by age**

**Example 2: a first-line chatbot replaces a call to the practice**

**Example 3: the retinal fundus is photographed at the visit**

**Example 4: Moscow, Russia — a model stands in as the second reader for mammograms**

## Speaker notes

The front door ended on an axis and a vocabulary: reading the data got cheaper, and the bill is paid in attention, skill, signature, and arbitration; nine forms of relocated work were handed out before their first use, so they could be called by one word from here on. And it ended on a question that will now be asked at every stage: the model looked first — what does the person do now, and who measured that. Here that question is asked for the first time, and it's asked at the very first stage of the path, because there's nothing before it: you can only look at someone who was invited. The woman with the letter by age, the one the lecture opened with, stands right here — she's been invited, and what follows is a look at who decides how to call people in, and whom to look at more closely.

The makeup of the section: four breakdowns of one piece of work from four different angles, and one of them is a failure — the first-line chatbot, the only case in the lecture where the test "take the model away" answered "the service keeps running." Each of the three takes up more than one slide: for the first, the cost of arbitration is counted separately; for the second, how it's built; for the third, both.

Further on the lecture examines decisions about a person who has already come into view, while here it's being decided whether they come into view at all — and that decision is made before any diagnosis, often with no doctor, and always with no participation from the patient at the moment it's made.

Four breakdowns show one piece of work from four sides. The Swedish program stood inside an already-running invitation channel and decided not "refer or not" but "who gets the second human reading"; it's examined through what a randomized trial measured, and through what it didn't measure at all — the construction of arbitration itself and the asymmetry of trust in a finding, as a separate slide right after. The first-line chatbot stood at the entry point of the flow of inquiries and decided whom to route where; it's examined through what was publicly claimed, and through what a cheapened entry point did to the channel's economics. The retinal fundus at the visit is a case where the payoff is measured not in reading accuracy but in turnout: 22 out of a hundred against a hundred out of a hundred. The numbers and bases are on the slides themselves.

What's common across the three breakdowns isn't the topic or the industry — it's what all three show: selection decides not just "who to treat" but, before that, "who's even worth noticing," and a mistake at this step isn't visible where it's usually looked for, because it happens before any diagnosis and before any accuracy measurement.

The photo alongside — a technician positioning a person at the mammography machine: the exact minute an invitation by age turns into a procedure. The woman with the letter stands right here, right now: the decision about her was made not by her and not by a doctor, but by a program — by her year of birth. The work of selection looks like this: not dramatic, but routine — and it's in that routineness that it most often goes unnoticed.
