**Real hardware, not a diagram.** The ring, alignment sensors, soft capture latches — this is exactly what physically receives Dragon on the final meters of approach.

**This is exactly where the precision named on the neighboring slide arrives.** Millimeter tolerance in angle and position is not an abstraction, but a requirement of this specific mechanism.

## Speaker notes

The photo shows the main components of the International Docking Adapter, the same NASA Docking System design that Dragon autonomously approaches over the final 220 meters. The photo was taken in 2016, before Crew Dragon's crewed flights began, and shows the mechanism on the ground, without a ship nearby — exactly the hardware the automation is aiming for.

The elements visible in the photo — the docking ring, alignment sensors, and soft capture latches — work as the final mechanical check on the automation's calculation: even a perfectly calculated approach trajectory must coincide with this specific physical ring within the given tolerance, or soft docking will not happen.

The neighboring breakdown already named the price of manual error — millisecond precision at the edge of human motor control. This photo makes that price literal: you must coincide not with an abstract point in space, but with a real mechanical component of a specific size, visible in the photo. It is to this mechanical precision, not to an arbitrarily chosen number, that the automation of the NASA Docking System, operating on Dragon since 2020, is calibrated.
