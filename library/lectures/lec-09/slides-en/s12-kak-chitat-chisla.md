## Speaker notes

Before we move on to the case studies, it's worth explicitly naming the rule by which this lecture reads quantitative claims — the same rule the course requires of any measurable claim: a number without a stated base doesn't read as anything, and a threshold is never confused with a fact.

Three types of numbers will come up repeatedly in the lecture, and each has its own reading rule. A fact-number is an event that has already happened and been measured: AEGIS's hit rate going from 24% to 93%, Dragon's routine docking since 2020, the Starcloud-1 launch on November 2, 2025. Such numbers always come with a source and a date. A threshold-number is a condition under which something will become possible, but hasn't yet: $200/kg for orbital computing to be competitive, <$100/kg as Starship's target. Such numbers are always explicitly marked as a plan, a goal, or a condition, not an achieved state — confusing these two types would mean believing that orbital data centers are already cheaper than ground-based ones today, when the honest material says the opposite. An analyst's-estimate-number is a range proposed by an independent observer, not by a participant in the events themselves: $100–500/kg from Aerospace Corporation, $115–200/kg from Ark Invest. Such numbers are always given as a range with a named source, not as one precise figure, because collapsing a range to one number for the sake of a tidy slide would distort the very nature of the estimate.

A separate rule concerns the denominator of any share or percentage. AEGIS's "93% hit rate" without stating "of what" means nothing — the lecture names it: of the total number of selected targets, compared against 24% for the previous mode. Starlink's "~65% of all active satellites" is of the total number of active satellites in orbit, not of the total number of spacecraft ever launched. Where the denominator can't be found in open sources, the lecture states that directly as a gap, rather than inventing a plausible-looking figure. This habit — asking "of exactly what" — will be useful for every one of the thirteen case studies ahead.
