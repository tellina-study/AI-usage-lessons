## Speaker notes

The care-delivery cycle is laid out here in full: nine stages, each with its own decision about a person, and three pillars that hold the order of the first of them. The stage names used are the ones the industry itself uses: an engineer walking into a medical project will hear exactly these words, and the conversation will start with them.

The first band is while deciding. **Primary screening and selection for examination** answers one question: whether to call this person in to look at all. Screening is examining people with no complaints, by a formal criterion, most often age and the interval since the last exam. **Instrumental and laboratory diagnostics** answers a different question: what has been measured about the person, and with what accuracy. **Diagnosis** brings together complaints, examination, and test results, and answers which illness explains what was observed and which test to order next.

The second band is decided and treating. **Prescribing therapy and dose titration** answers what to treat with, how much, and whom it's fine not to treat; the dose is refined against measurements, and this adjustment is repeated many times until the indicator settles within the target range. **Surgical intervention and intraoperative monitoring** decides whether to operate, by what approach, and what to do on the table when the picture turns out to be different from what was expected.

The third band is after the decision. **Ongoing monitoring and clinical alerts** answers a question that repeats: intervene now, or on the next round. What's read here is the direction of the measurement trend: pulse on its own says little; a rising pulse with falling pressure says a lot. **Medical record-keeping** answers a question about the next reader: what should the person who wasn't at the visit, and who will be the one making the next decision from the record, learn.

Two stages don't fit into this row, and lining them up in it would be a misrepresentation. **Clearance, oversight, and accountability** is a procedure that stands around every stage at once: a signature under a report as a legal act, informed consent, a case review after the outcome, clearing a device for use and overseeing it after clearance. The decision this stage makes sounds like this: who is answerable for what was done, and what changes in the order of work when a review finds a discrepancy. It cannot be placed as a separate step in the cycle. **Professional qualification: upkeep and wear** is a measurement of the person themselves: whether they're staying in shape. The profession doesn't ask the doctor about this — it counts on their behalf, through practice volume, accreditation, and recertification. No step of the work itself shows this measurement at all.

And one more stage stands before the whole cycle begins. **Drug development** answers the question of where the thing being prescribed at the prescribing stage even comes from in the first place: a target in the body, a molecule that acts on it, preclinical work on cell cultures and animals, then phases of trials on people.

Now about the order, because it isn't decorative and it isn't a convenience of exposition. The order of the first stages is held by three pillars, and each can be checked.

**Who was invited.** You can only look at someone who came into view. A person who wasn't invited is seen by no system, however accurate it is. So the decision about whom to invite is made before all the others, and it determines who even becomes the subject of the decisions that follow.

**Which group the numbers were computed on.** The group on which a measurement's properties are computed is assembled by the selection stage. The rates used to describe any "sick or not" system are computed on different groups of people: one on those who have the disease, another on those who don't. The share of true alerts among all the alerts raised depends on how prevalent the disease is in the group examined, and it is not a property of the device at all. The same system, in a mass screening and in a specialized department, will place a completely different picture on the doctor's desk.

**The threshold for "sick."** The point past which the system declares "sick" is chosen by protocol, priced against the outcome — against a price set before any accuracy at all: which is worse, missing a finding or calling in someone unnecessarily. That choice is made before the developer, and the developer does not overrule it.

Further on, the order holds the same way. You can only decide on what has been measured. Monitoring only makes sense after a decision has been made. You can only read a record after it has been made. There is no reordering under which this still works.
