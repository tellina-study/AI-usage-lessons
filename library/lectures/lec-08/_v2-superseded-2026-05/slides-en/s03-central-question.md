# The lecture's central question

## Assertion

What has AI done to the creative industry by 2026 — and where is an engineer wise to say "AI is not needed here"?

## Visual

Central slide with the question in an Ocean rounded box (large, occupying the center of the slide). The question is split into 2 visually separated halves. The first half — the gold-highlighted "what AI has done" — points to the content of Sections 1-3 (added/changed/broke). The second half — the teal-highlighted "where to say 'no'" — points to the content of Sections 4-5 (refusal criteria + checklist). Below the question in small type — a pointer: "Two-part by design — both parts are equally important."

## Speaker notes

The central question of this lecture is this: what has AI done to the creative industry by 2026 — and where is an engineer wise to say "AI is not needed here." This question is two-part by design: both parts are equally important and both will be unfolded over the course of the lecture. The first part — "what AI has done" — unfolds through the keystone axis ADDED-CHANGED-BROKE, which structures the first three sections. This is a diagnostic block: which capabilities were added, how they changed the pipeline and the economics, what legal and reputational debt accumulated in parallel. The second part — "where to say 'no'" — is a normative block that culminates in a concrete checklist of negative-choice criteria. We do not teach students to deploy AI into any creative task — we teach them to recognize which tasks it is wise to put AI into and which to refuse it before the start. These two halves — the diagnostic and the normative — are parallel and equally important for qualified engineering judgment. If, by the end of the lecture, a student can name four concrete criteria for refusing AI in a creative project, they will carry away practical value that does not depend on which model they will be integrating a year from now.
