## Speaker notes

The hardware really is flying and really is answering real requests — this isn't a concept on paper or a render of a future product. At the same time, the scale is honestly tiny: a handful of chips against tens of thousands of GPUs in ground-based data centers. This is the start of a new infrastructure category, not a replacement for the existing one, and the breakdowns that follow say exactly that, without overstating it.

This section flips the logic the whole previous lecture ran on, and that reversal is worth naming explicitly. In Sections 1–3, the question was "what part of a human's or a classical method's work can a model do, physically present where the action happens." In Section 4, the question is different: not what the model can do, but where it's physically cheaper to put it, if the task itself (language-model inference) isn't tied to any particular point in space at all. A task performed by a language model can, in principle, run anywhere there's electricity and cooling — which is exactly why the question of placement becomes a purely engineering question of power and cooling economics, not a question about the model's own capabilities.

This is an honest reversal worth keeping in mind through both of the next breakdowns: Starcloud and Suncatcher prove the principle — the hardware physically works in space — not economic competitiveness. The difference between "it works" and "it pays off" is the same honesty the whole industry overview held onto with the $200/kg threshold number: the threshold is far off, but it's named, not invented.
