# Visible content

## Title bar
Discovery in plain terms

## Body
**What it is.** The phase where you're searching not for a solution but for a problem: who hurts, how badly, and whether the person is willing to pay for it — in time, reputation, or money.

**Why.** You can build anything; only what solves a real pain of a real person has value. The guess "I would use something like this" is not a fact.

**Mental model.** There are no facts inside the building — facts are outside. You write down a hypothesis, go out to people, and check it. AI speeds up the collection, but doesn't replace the conversation with a real person.

## Speaker notes

Before moving to the formal methods, let's break down the research phase in simple terms — so it's clear even to those hearing about product work for the first time.

Research is the loop's first arrow. Here you're not building anything yet. Your only task is to understand whether it's worth building anything at all, and for whom. It sounds simple, but this exact step is where most products fail: the team falls in love with its own idea and builds it without checking whether anyone has a real problem that idea solves.

The key mental model worth taking away from this section: there are no facts inside the building. Everything you think about users while sitting at your desk is a hypothesis, not knowledge. Facts live outside, with real people. So the discipline of research is the discipline of going outside: formulate what you believe, then honestly test it on people who have no interest in flattering you.

AI can help a lot here — summarizing interviews, gathering desk research in minutes instead of hours. But it can't have the conversation with a real person for you, and it can't produce real disagreement. Next we'll see two classical methods that make this conversation honest, and three real failures where research was replaced by a plausible fabrication.
