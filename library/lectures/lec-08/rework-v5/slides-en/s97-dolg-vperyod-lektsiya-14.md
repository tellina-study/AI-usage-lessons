# Questions

## Speaker notes

One topic is named today and deliberately left unopened. It is more honest to say so directly than to leave the impression that the evening closed the subject entirely. When discernment stops confirming itself, what appears is not only the cost of verification — the one we counted in the section on recognition and provenance — but an entire class of attacks that live exactly on that cost. Identity forgery. Voice fraud, where the caller speaks in a voice the person on the other end has known for twenty years. Bypassing hiring checks, when the candidate on a video interview turns out not to be the person who was hired. Recognizers placed inside a security loop, with all the consequences of what we measured about them today.

This is the subject of a separate lecture, and the recognizer will return in it in a different role. Today it was a measuring instrument, and we held it to that standard: what share of decisions are correct at the working threshold on the distribution that will actually be in play, and why the area under the curve stays respectable at exactly the point where working accuracy has dropped to chance. There it will become an element of a security system, and the questions put to it will be different: where to set the threshold, how much a false alarm costs, how much a miss costs, and who the person is who makes the decision on its say-so and answers for that decision.

The image on this frame is a short illustration of exactly what this is about. In January 2025, while wildfires were burning in California, an image of the Hollywood sign on fire spread across social media. The sign was not on fire. The image had been generated, and it was taken for footage from the scene. The viewer's attentiveness settles nothing here: there is no longer a free way to tell one from the other. Someone now has to pay for exactly that task.

What's worth taking away is what the measurement actually produced. Execution in creative production really did stop being the scarce resource, and that is good news: for the first time in sixty years of getting cheaper, it became cheap to be wrong. Seventy thousand trials instead of one stopped being a budget question. The work did not disappear because of this — it moved onto the step that didn't get cheaper, and that step is called selection. The skill of picking one variant out of seventy thousand, measuring the win against the classical mode, and saying out loud "the model is not the right tool here" — that is exactly the part of the craft the tool has not taken away, and will not take away any time soon.

Why this is a genuine debt, and not something a polite closing reference can discharge. The recognizer we measured today on training material is already standing in working security loops: it is used to check uploaded documents, video calls in remote hiring, support requests. It carries today's number there with it — ninety-nine point nine percent on a familiar family and fifty-one point nine on an unfamiliar one, while the area under the curve stays respectable in both cases. A security system built on an area-under-the-curve report will look reliable right up until the first attack by a model the recognizer has never seen. This is no longer a conversation about the creative industries, and it needs to be taken apart separately and in full.

And one last thing, already beyond the creative industries. The technique we used today carries over anywhere. When the price of some step in a piece of work drops to nearly zero, ask three things in a row: what exactly came to cost fractions of a cent; who now does the work that used to happen on its own; and what the win is being compared against when someone names it. Three questions — and most of the attractive numbers about model adoption fall apart before you've spent a day checking them.
