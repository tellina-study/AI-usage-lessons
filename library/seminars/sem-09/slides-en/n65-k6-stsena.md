# What Olga does with the photo in a request

## Assertion

The client types the amount as text and attaches a photo of the same payment. Today, only Olga cross-checks these two numbers against each other, with her eyes.

## Visual

> The attachment — a receipt, transfer, or banking-app screenshot; the same form has a text field for the amount, in the client's own words.

1. Matches — the request moves on. Doesn't match — Olga doesn't decide which number is right: she sets the request aside and asks the client to confirm.

> **What it costs the center if the cross-check is removed.** The photo and the text are two independent ways of learning the same number. An unchecked discrepancy surfaces only later, at reconciliation — by which point the reschedule is already made.

> Olga: "I don't guess from a photo. If a figure isn't completely clear, I ask again. The amount is decided once — redoing it costs more than one extra question."

## Speaker notes

The training center takes in requests for payment and for rescheduling classes through a form, and some of the requests arrive with an attachment. The client attaches a photo of a receipt, a transfer, or a screenshot of a banking app — any proof that the payment really went through. The same form has an ordinary text field where they write the amount in their own words: "I paid eighteen thousand nine hundred ninety rubles," or just the digits.

Today, Olga is the one who works with this pair — the photo and the text — and she works the way any human would with a document in hand. She opens the submitted photo, zooms in on the relevant part if needed, and reads the amount and the date with her eyes. This isn't a separate strict procedure with a protocol — it's exactly the same reading Olga uses for any piece of paper on her desk.

She doesn't take what she reads on faith by itself. She compares the number from the photo with the number the client typed as text in the same request. If they match, there's no question, the request moves on. If they don't match — and a phone shot can always be misread, the light falls however it falls, the paper is crumpled — she doesn't resolve that discrepancy herself and doesn't pick which of the two numbers is closer to the truth. She sets the request aside and asks the client directly.

This is worth pausing on. The photo and the request's text are two ways of learning the same number, independent of each other: one is written by the client's hand, the other is shot by a phone off the document. Averaging them doesn't smooth out a discrepancy — it's a signal that one of the two has an error. A decision made on one of them without comparing it against the second sends that error forward unnoticed, and it's only caught when payments are reconciled at month's end — by which point the reschedule is already made, and fixing it costs more.

Olga herself talks about this habit calmly and plainly: she doesn't give an unclear figure on a blurry shot the benefit of her own confidence — she asks again. The decision on the amount is made once, and there's usually no second attempt at it.
