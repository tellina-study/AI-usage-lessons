# Three Questions, Thirty Repeats, Three Kinds of Unfitness to Send

## Assertion

The same process produces three different kinds of defect on three hard questions — and none of the three is visible from the run log.

## Visual

> **Methodology.** Node `Model (T = 0)`, ten repeats per question.

> **"10 out of 10" at the boundary — UNIFORMLY WRONG**: this isn't a success — all ten land on "through the manager," even though 3,000 ₽ isn't above the threshold.

> None of the findings is visible in the log — seeing it takes reading the content.

## Speaker notes

There's no new screen in the process schema here — we're looking at what the already-assembled node gives on thirty real calls. Three questions, ten identical repeats each, temperature zero, the same knowledge base in the prompt.

The first question — arithmetic inside the answer: a client paid for ten classes, attended three, and asks for a refund on the rest. There's no ready-made formula for this case in the knowledge base — the model has to either genuinely do the math or hand the calculation off to a human. Ten identical requests produced three different treatments: some answers name the client an exact amount, some name no number at all and send them to the administrator, and one answer talks about an entirely different rule from the knowledge base, as if asked about transferring to another course. Three different outcomes out of ten — on an absolutely identical question.

The second question sits exactly on the rule's boundary: the refund amount equals the threshold, literally — not more, not less. And here — the complete opposite: ten out of ten repeats pick the same branch, "the decision goes through the manager." The problem is that this is wrong: the rule says "for amounts above three thousand," and three thousand is not above three thousand. Not one flip, and yet a wrong answer ten times running. One of these ten repeats (exec 816) goes even further than a simple application error: it doesn't quote the client's amount — it replaces it with "more than 3,000 rubles," bending what the client actually wrote to fit the decision it had already made. This isn't a typo: the input is reworded to match a ready-made conclusion, and the only way to notice it is to already know, in advance, what the client actually wrote. The 3,000-ruble threshold isn't invented fresh here: this session's build imports it from the previous session's code (`REFUND_THRESHOLD = s8.REFUND_THRESHOLD`). In the previous session, the comparison against this threshold lives in code. Here, in the run version, the same comparison is decided by the model, reading the text of the rule — and that's exactly the comparison it just failed.

The third question combines two knowledge-base items that pull in different directions. The set of facts the model names is perfectly stable — zero discrepancies out of ten. But the answer's own text contradicts itself: the first sentence says "there will be no free retake," and the following ones directly name the date and place to check the retake schedule. A client who reads the whole thing will conclude they can show up — and be wrong.

For comparison — a question whose answer sits whole in the knowledge base: twenty-five repeats, five phrasings, zero substantive discrepancies. The difference isn't in the model and isn't in the settings — it's in whether the answer needs to be found or composed.

None of the three findings is visible in any log: the runs are green, there are no process errors, every answer is syntactically correct.
