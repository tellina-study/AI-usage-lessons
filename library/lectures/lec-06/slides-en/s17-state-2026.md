# Surrogates-2026: "up to N×" is an upper bound, not typical

## Assertion

By 2026, major vendors have turned surrogates into a product; claimed speedups of "up to N×" are an upper bound on a narrow class of problems, not the typical case.

## Visual

Three product cards side by side in an Ocean rounded box, each with a small vendor logo (24px). Card 1 — "Ansys SimAI" (2026 R1 release): "a surrogate based on a reduced-order model · a claimed speedup of tens to hundreds of times." Card 2 — "Altair PhysicsAI": "trained on a historical archive of simulations · a claimed speedup of up to on the order of a thousand times." Card 3 — "NVIDIA PhysicsNeMo": "an open Python physics-AI framework · from PINNs to neural operators." Numbers are given as "up to ~10²–10³×" (a range), not exact. At the bottom — a wide gold plate (`#F0AB00` bold, 16pt): "N× is on a case favorable to the vendor; the cost of generating the training archive is excluded from the ROI." No more than four product names on the slide.

## Speaker notes

By 2026, major vendors had turned surrogates into a product. Let's list them briefly, reading the numbers critically. Ansys SimAI, in the 2026 R1 release — a surrogate based on a reduced-order model, interpolating between previously computed results; the claimed speedup — on the order of tens to hundreds of times on compute-heavy problems. Altair PhysicsAI is trained on a historical archive of simulations and deployed as a "solver," with a claimed speedup of up to on the order of a thousand times. NVIDIA PhysicsNeMo — an open Python framework for physics-AI, covering a spectrum from pure PINNs to neural operators. Similar surrogate models for specific problem classes, such as turbomachinery, are also announced by other vendor partnerships, with claimed speedups on the order of thousands of times.

A methodological remark is needed here, and it matters more than the numbers themselves. Each of these speedups is "up to N times" on a case favorable to the vendor. Averaged across a fleet of problems, the gain is more modest, and almost always the return-on-investment calculation excludes the cost of generating the training archive — and that means thousands of expensive runs of the exact solver that someone had to pay for in advance. Numbers of the "a thousand times" kind should be treated as an upper bound on a narrow class of problems, not as a typical speedup. This is a direct illustration of a general rule of the course: a marketing number is a ceiling on a convenient case, while an engineering decision is made based on the tool's typical behavior, not its records.
