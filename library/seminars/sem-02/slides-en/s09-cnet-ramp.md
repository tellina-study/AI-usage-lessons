# When a formula was needed — and when AI closed a gap nothing else could

## Assertion

CNET: 41 of 77 AI-written articles needed corrections, including an arithmetic
error. Ramp: coverage went from 1.5% to nearly complete in seconds

## Visual

Two contrasting cards. Left (red accent): "CNET, January 2023" — of 77
AI-generated finance articles, 41 needed corrections; example error: "$10,000 at
3% annual interest earns $10,300 in a year" instead of the correct $300 in
earnings. Right (green accent): "Ramp" — request coverage went from 1.5-3%
manual to close to 100%, under 10 seconds per request, ~99% of decisions
correct, ~25% of requests the agent rejects.

## Speaker notes

Two cases from real practice. In January 2023 it came out that the outlet CNET
had been publishing finance articles generated by an AI tool for several
months. Of 77 such articles, 41 needed corrections. The flagship example of an
error: one of the articles claimed that "10,000 dollars at 3% annual interest
earns 10,300 dollars in a year" — instead of the correct 300 dollars in
earnings, the model confused the total amount with the earnings themselves, a
basic arithmetic error. The lesson specific to this one: the calculation should
be done by a deterministic formula, not an LLM "eyeballing it" — wherever exact
arithmetic is needed, the model should replace the text around the calculator,
not the calculator itself. What went wrong here, in your view? Now the positive
example: an engineering blog post from the company Ramp describes how requests
to fix transaction classification used to be handled manually, with coverage of
only 1.5-3% of requests — meaning the overwhelming majority went unprocessed.
After rolling out an LLM agent — under 10 seconds per request, around 99% of
decisions judged correct, around 25% of requests the agent rejects. It's
important to say plainly: this isn't a win for AI over deterministic code, it's
a replacement for manual processing that had negligible coverage.
