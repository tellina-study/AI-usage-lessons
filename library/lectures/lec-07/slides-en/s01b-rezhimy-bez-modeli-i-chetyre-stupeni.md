## Visible slide text

**The regulator's requirement: the person is the last link.** The European rule puts it more precisely: they must retain the capacity to disagree and overrule. It does not require presence at every single decision.

**How decisions are made without a model — the classical regimes, by name:**

- **Selection** — double reading by two independents, arbitration by a third on disagreement.
- **Diagnostics** — two rates at once: how many were found among the sick and how many of the healthy weren't needlessly alarmed, both at the protocol's threshold.
- **Diagnosis** — write out what could explain it, order the test that distinguishes between them; a second opinion, a case conference.
- **Monitoring** — an early warning score: points, a sum, a threshold for escalation.
- **Documentation** — record structure, proofreading, signature, discharge summary.
- **Oversight** — a case review after the outcome, and a change to the order of work.

## Speaker notes

All the work below existed before any automation, and exists now in the same form. The model enters an order that is already in place; it does not build one of its own, and that is why the conversation about it begins with how that order itself is built.

Briefly, about where data on a person even comes from, because there are six ways, and they give different things. A visit to a doctor gives complaints, history, and examination — what the person said and what the doctor saw with their own hands. An instrumental study gives values taken by a device: a plain scan, a tomogram, an endoscopy. A lab test gives blood, urine, tissue, and culture readings — and some of them come with their own built-in delay that nothing removes: a sputum culture takes three weeks. A remote study is set up so that the person is scanned in one place and the image is read by a specialist somewhere else; this is what radiology diagnostics rests on wherever there is no radiologist of their own. Reviewing documents already on file adds no new measurement at all — it extracts from what's already recorded and becomes a source of decisions in its own right. And a sixth way, which over the past two decades has become commonplace: measurements the patient takes themselves — a glucose monitor, a home blood-pressure cuff, a symptom diary. These same six ways are how data is obtained wherever a model works alongside too: the model doesn't change how the data is obtained, it changes who decides on it, and how.

Now the classical regimes, and they have to be called by name: the model will have to be compared against exactly these, and a model compared against an empty space always wins, and that win means nothing.

In selection, double reading is at work. The scan is read by two people, and the word "independently" is load-bearing here: the moment the second reader sees the first one's conclusion, two opinions stop being two — the second reader starts answering "do I agree," which is a different task with a different error rate. The payoff of double reading comes from the mismatch of errors: the two people's misses land on different scans. Arbitration resolves a disagreement — a third reader, brought in only on disagreement, who wrote neither of the two conclusions. In diagnostics, a pair of rates is at work: of everyone who has the disease, what share did the system call sick, and of everyone who doesn't, what share it didn't needlessly alarm. These two rates are computed on different groups of people and are always stated as a pair, because they move together: shift the threshold, and one rises while the other falls. That's why they're given for one threshold, and the threshold is chosen by protocol, priced against the outcome. In establishing a diagnosis, differential diagnosis is at work: writing out the diseases that could explain what's observed, and ordering the test that will tell them apart; the order of the tests is a separate decision, because some of them are themselves dangerous or expensive. Alongside sit a second opinion, a case conference, and clinical guidelines. In monitoring, an early warning score is at work: every measured value gets points for deviating from normal, the points are added up, the sum is compared against a threshold, and the threshold dictates the action. Two of its properties: the points pass between shifts, while the judgment "I think he's taken a turn" does not; and the weights in the table are set by people and published. In documentation, record structure is at work — complaints, history, examination, diagnosis, plan — proofreading, and a signature. And over all of it stands oversight: a case review after the outcome, and a change to the order of work as a result.

What all these regimes have in common is one thing, and it's worth carrying into any other industry. Each one exists because one person makes mistakes, and the procedure already knows that. The procedure doesn't make people more attentive — it's built so that one person's mistake is caught before it becomes irreversible, or reviewed after it already has.

The ladder on the slide is the criterion by which all the evening's breakdowns are sorted: who looks first. Looks first — the person reads the data themselves, and the model is brought in afterward, or not at all; this is the classical regime the model gets compared against. Looks second — the first pass is made by the model, and the person reads its result and either confirms or rejects it. Looks only at disputed cases — the person is brought in only where the model and a second source disagree; they never see the rest of the flow. Signs without looking — the requirement is formally met, the signature is there, but there's no actual participation behind it. The ladder doesn't rank the rungs by quality: each one has both a payoff and its own blind spot. But the gap between the first rung and the fourth is bigger than the gap between any two models: an argument about whose model is more accurate happens inside one cell, while the move from "looks first" to "signs without looking" changes the procedure itself.

Why it is the person who signs, and not the model — three reasons, and accuracy isn't among them: it's measured that they are often not more accurate, and a case for them resting on their accuracy would collapse at the very first breakdown. The right to sign is granted to them: in none of the countries examined does a model hold the right to sign, and in a case review it is the person's actions that get evaluated. They err differently: the payoff of a pair comes from the mismatch of errors, not from one side's superiority. A decision already made cannot be undone: a device gets recalled, and recalled for real, while decisions made on it before the recall stay as they were.

And three checks that work beyond medicine too; the evening will come back to them at the end. First: if a conclusion is checked against the model's own self-explanation, that's not a check — the explanation is built by the same system and is aligned with the conclusion by construction. Second: you need two rates at once — the share of suggestions rejected, and the share of correct rejections; without the second, the first confirms nothing, since you could reject everything. Third: can the person physically keep up — seconds available for the decision against the volume of the flow; sometimes the answer to this is "no," and later it will be given a number. A loop that fails these three checks is exactly the fourth rung: the signature is there, and no participation stands behind it.
