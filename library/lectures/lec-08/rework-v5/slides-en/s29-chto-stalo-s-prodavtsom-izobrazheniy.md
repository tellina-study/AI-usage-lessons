## Visible slide text

**Subscribers: 321,000 → 240,000 in a year. Debt ≈$2.1 billion, bankruptcy — October 2026.**

**When a channel gets cheaper, whoever buys it survives. When the unit itself loses its value, there's nothing to buy: it has no owner.**

## Speaker notes

The section's first case showed what the per-unit price collapse delivered. This slide is the
other half of the same pair: what it demanded, and from whom it took. The unit is the same — a
dollar per one usable picture.

It's worth starting with the unit's anchor: a retail license cost $130–499, wholesale by
subscription — ≈$2.50. Against generation's $0,034–0.05, that's a gap from 73 times to wholesale
up to 14,700 to retail. The entire business model stood on the premise that a finished image
costs money because someone shot it. And right away, an honest gap that explains why this slide
is built exactly this way: there's no measured year-over-year series of the illustrator's own
rate in the sources. The level of classical rates is known, the trend isn't measured, and
plugging in an estimate here isn't allowed — the rule works here against the convenient
conclusion, not for it. So the collapse is shown where it's actually counted — on the image
seller's balance sheet.

And there it's counted in detail. The stock agency's subscribers over the year: 321,000 against
240,000, that is, −25%; revenue −2.5%, down to $229.1 million. The merger with the largest
competitor was called off on July 7, 2026, after the British regulator made the sale of the
acquired company's entire editorial business a condition. Trading in its stock was halted on
September 29. A pre-arranged bankruptcy was filed on October 12–13 with debt of ≈$2.1 billion; the
owning family and a partner contribute up to $533 million, creditors write off $270 million. The
company itself named the reason: growing pressure from model-generated images — the company's own
wording in a corporate statement, not an analyst's conclusion. The earlier popular formulation,
"the two largest stock agencies are consolidating in response to artificial intelligence," has to
be retired: the merger didn't happen, and this has to be said differently — the attempt to
protect itself didn't work.

The historical counterpoint turns the story from news into a lesson, and that's exactly why this
slide sits in the section the way it does — as a comparison of two maneuvers by the same player,
twenty years apart. 2006: the classical agencies sold an image for $100–1000s, microstock for
$0.25–1. The owner of the expensive library bought the microstock, that is, its own
price-breaker. The market split into "cheap and plentiful" and "expensive and exactly this"; the
upper segment survived, and the buyer stayed the dominant player in it. The maneuver worked
because microstock was a NEW CHANNEL for selling the exact same thing — a photograph shot by a
human; a channel can be bought, it has an owner, a contract, a price.

2026: the same player tries to merge with a competitor. The maneuver didn't work, and the
regulator has nothing to do with it: even a completed merger of two photo sellers wouldn't have
brought back the unit price, because it wasn't their market share that lost value, it was the
product itself. The difference comes down to one line: generation is the ABSENCE OF A THING that
needs licensing. You can't buy its owner, because there is no owner: there's no company you could
acquire the right from for people to stop generating pictures, and there's no contract that could
formalize that right. When a channel gets cheaper, whoever buys the channel survives. When the
unit itself loses its value, there's nothing to buy.

For a student there's a practical conclusion here, not gloating. Before planning a response to a
price drop, ask what exactly got cheaper: the product's delivery channel, or the product itself.
A cheapened channel gets bought, intercepted, or bypassed — it belongs to someone, you can
negotiate with it. A unit that's lost its value isn't fixed by buying anything at all: the only
move is to find what in your work isn't that unit, and move the price there. For the image
seller, that remainder turned out to be the editorial archive — photographs of specific events,
which by definition don't exist in generated form — and it's telling that this was exactly what
the regulator demanded be sold off, and the deal fell apart over it. All three surviving products
visible in this story — rights to an image with confirmed provenance, the editorial archive,
access to the filming of an event that happened once — share one property: their value doesn't
rest on the image as such, but on there being a checkable fact standing behind it. For a
freelancer, that remainder will most likely turn out to be selection and accountability for what
ships — exactly what's moved to the right all evening. This leads straight into the next pair of
slides, about what these models are actually trained on, and what can be released from their
output.
