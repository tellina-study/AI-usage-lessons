## Visible slide text

10 years ago (2015), **not a single** company in the world had demonstrated a successful first-stage landing.

## Speaker notes

The numbers on falling launch cost and the growing number of satellites don't say who is behind this or how much the industry itself has accelerated — and those are two separate questions, easy to lose behind the raw totals.

Reusability is no longer the privilege of one company, though landing and actual reuse are worth separating explicitly. As of the end of October 2026, four companies worldwide have demonstrated their own first-stage landing technology. Two have confirmed landing plus actual reuse: SpaceX (routinely since 2017) and Blue Origin (New Glenn, first landing November 13, 2025, first reuse April 19, 2026). Two more have demonstrated successful landing without confirmed reuse — both from China: LandSpace and Galactic Energy. Ten years ago, in 2015, not a single company in the world had demonstrated a successful landing at all.

At the same time, concentration remains high: according to independent aggregate statistics, since the start of 2026, of 254 orbital launches, 150 went to the US, 76 to China, 13 to Russia. More than half of the world's 2026 launches belong to one country, mostly through SpaceX, and that concentration shouldn't be hidden behind the phrase "14 countries can launch".

The commercial sector has overtaken the government sector by revenue: per an OECD review, commercial space-industry revenue in 2026 is $544.3 billion, more than 80% of the entire global space economy. Ten years ago the ratio was reversed — most of the revenue came from government contracts.

And the pace of iteration has grown not just in launches per year, but in the turnaround between two flights of the same vehicle: the record turnaround for a single booster between landing and its next launch is nine days. Before 2017, this metric couldn't have had a numerical value at all — every stage flew once and was discarded.
