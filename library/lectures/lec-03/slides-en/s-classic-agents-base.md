# Visible content

## Title bar
"Controlled automation existed long before agents"

## Body
[Three classical-automation cards, Ocean rounded box]

**Finite-state machine** — a set of states and transition rules on events ("request created → in processing → closed"). Possible transitions are visible by definition, impossible ones are excluded by construction.

**Workflow engines** — execute a pre-described process along a fixed scheme: BPMN (business-process notation), DAG orchestrators (Airflow), RPA (routine robotization). The order of steps is defined in advance.

**Control loop** — plan → act → check as a feedback loop from control theory and process control; the military analogue is the OODA loop (observe–orient–decide–act). Not a single action, but a loop.

[Gold callout — what to keep from the classics]
**What to keep: deterministic workflows where possible; idempotency (repeating a step does not break the state); the principle of least privilege (least-privilege) for tools; limits and audit of the loop. The reliability practices of the loop do not go anywhere.**

[Bridge, bottom]
An LLM agent is the same control loop plan→act→check, but its steps are generated by the model non-deterministically rather than selected by a fixed rule of a state machine. Hence both the flexibility and the failures of the section.

## Speaker notes

The word "agent" sounds like an invention of the era of large models, but controlled process automation is a mature engineering discipline with many years of history, and without it you cannot honestly assess what an LLM agent adds and what it breaks. Let us restore the baseline from scratch. The simplest classical model of controlled behavior is the finite-state machine: a system is described by a finite set of states and rules of transition between them on events. Request created, in processing, approved or rejected, closed — this is a finite-state machine; in each state a strictly defined set of transitions is allowed. The behavior is fully predictable and checkable: impossible transitions are excluded by construction, not by hope.

When a process is more complex than a single state machine, classical engineering applies workflow engines, orchestrators — systems that execute a pre-described multi-step process along a fixed scheme. Let us name them concretely: BPMN — a standard for the graphical description of a process executed by an engine; Airflow-like orchestrators execute a process as a directed acyclic graph of tasks; RPA automates routine on top of existing interfaces along a rigid script. One thing unites all of these: the sequence of steps is defined in advance, not decided on the fly. Such a pipeline is deterministic, auditable, and repeatable.

The third classical idea, and it is exactly the one that leads to the agent loop, is the control loop plan→act→check as a feedback loop. This is a direct inheritance from control theory and robotics: a system measures the state of the environment, computes an action, applies it, measures again, and corrects. The military-engineering analogue is the OODA loop. The essence is one: not a single action, but a loop — plan, do, check, correct.

An LLM agent is the same control loop, but its steps are generated by the model non-deterministically rather than selected by a fixed rule of a state machine. Hence both the power and the fragility are visible: a deterministic loop always makes a predictable step, whereas a model can invent a step that was not in the scheme. And the classical reliability practices — determinism where possible, idempotency, limits, audit, least privilege — do not go anywhere.
