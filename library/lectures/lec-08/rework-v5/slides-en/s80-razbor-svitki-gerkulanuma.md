## Speaker notes

The one case in the lecture where the win has no downside. It is broken down by the same five-step form as the others.

The cost of both errors is named, and it is asymmetric. In the year 79, the eruption of Vesuvius buried the villa together with its library; the fire did not destroy the papyrus scrolls — cut off from air, they charred and survived as fragile carbon cylinders. The cost of trying to read them the classical way is the destruction of the object: unrolling a charred scroll means crumbling it. Over two and a half centuries, part of the collection has been lost this way; damage from attempts in the nineteenth century and the 1980s is still documented today. The cost of not trying is that the text is never read. There is no monetary scale for this pair, and that is a property of the task, not a gap in the selection: the object exists in a single copy, and both errors are equally final.

There is exactly one classical mode here, and this is fundamental. Physical unrolling destroys the scroll; every other reading method requires access to a surface that a rolled-up cylinder does not have. The alternative to machine reading is exactly one: never read it at all. This situation has to be distinguished from one where a classical mode exists but hasn't been counted: there, a number is missing; here, the mode itself is missing, and these are different things for counting a win.

The win here is not counted as a multiplier, because there is nothing to divide by: the classical mode gives zero scrolls read, by construction. It is counted in objects — one scroll in full, about twenty-two columns, for the first time in roughly two thousand years.

The whole breakdown rests on one thing: not a single letter was generated. The model does not fill in what's lost, does not guess at endings, does not reconstruct damaged spots by inferring meaning. It answers the question "where is the ink here," and that is a task with a checkable answer. The distinction between restoration and invention is held here absolutely, and held by the construction of the task, not by the goodwill of those carrying it out.

What moved downstream. What moved was the boundary of the possible, not the cost. Work that did not exist came into existence; no one lost a job, no stage became more expensive. Almost nothing moved downstream — and this rarity has to be named precisely as a rarity, or the case will be read as the norm.

A reminder of why this case has no multiplier, and why that absence is not a weakness of the breakdown. No classical mode producing the same result exists: unrolling the scroll means destroying it, and there is no other way to reach the surface of a rolled-up charred cylinder. There is nothing to divide by. The win here is measured not in multiples but in the fact that the set of readable texts stopped being empty.

This is the one case of the evening where the win has no named downside — and that is exactly why it belongs in a lecture otherwise occupied with cost. No specialist lost work: there are now more philologists reading Herculaneum scrolls, not fewer, because material appeared. No boundary shifted silently: the object remained intact, the result is checkable, zero was invented.

And what moved has to be named precisely. What moved was not the cost but the boundary of the possible. Work that did not exist in nature came into existence; two frames from now, this case will be set alongside "The Wizard of Oz," where the technique is a close neighbor and the outcome is the opposite.

And one more feature that makes this case suited to a classroom breakdown. The object exists in a single copy, and both errors — trying and not trying — are equally final. There is no monetary scale for such a pair and cannot be — that is a property of the task. Where both errors are irreversible, arithmetic does not choose — a person chooses, deciding which risk is acceptable.
