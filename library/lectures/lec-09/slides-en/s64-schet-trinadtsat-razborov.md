## Speaker notes

This paragraph holds the main conclusion of the whole lecture, and it's worth reading as an answer to a practical engineering question: when designing any new autonomous system for a frontier, it's useful to ask in advance which part of the decision cycle will go to the model, and which part is reserved for a mandatory classical check.

The thread running through the lecture: a decision that used to require a human present on site, or hours of their own calculation, is now made locally by a model or by automation. The recurring number grows from section to section: a multiple-fold payoff in Section 1, routine autonomy in Section 2, an order-of-magnitude payoff in Section 3, a recorded fact in Section 4.

The lecture's main count is a different one, and it matters more than any single number: thirteen cases were broken down in the lecture, and in all thirteen, the final check or the right to intervene stays with a classical, non-AI step, while in twelve of the thirteen the trained model does substantive work rather than just confirming it isn't needed. Let's name all thirteen, so the claim is checkable: AEGIS has a target profile set in advance; SPOC has the human driver as the final authority; COROB-X has the EaRLaP pipeline and an after-the-fact check; LeoLabs leaves the decision to maneuver with the operator; Dragon has manual intervention; predictive latency compensation leaves the decision to the operator; Astrobee has a classical controller that refines it; the Raptor surrogate has the test stand and CFD; the rover's route has a digital twin with 500,000 variables; AIFS/AIGEFS has classical data assimilation; generative design has engineering qualification before flight; Starcloud and Suncatcher have the decisions made by people at the design stage. In not one of the thirteen was a trained model trusted with the whole cycle.
