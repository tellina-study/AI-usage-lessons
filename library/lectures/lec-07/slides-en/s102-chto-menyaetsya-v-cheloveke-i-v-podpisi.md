**The four payments from the start — by name, with a measurement under each.**

| payment | what it cost | how it was measured |
|---|---|---|
| **Attention** | scanned area of the image **11.1 → 9.5%**; missed cancers **29 → 61** out of a hundred | eye-tracking study; subgroup of 14 cases |
| **Skill** | findings **284 → 224** per thousand procedures, same nineteen doctors | observational, measured once, and disputed |
| **Signature** | **9 of 11** machine-written discharge summaries contained an error | one hundred hospitalizations, two blind readers |
| **Arbitration** | readings sent to arbitration up **threefold and by a quarter**; one review takes **as long as five readings** | two centers, 50,000 women; 441,276 cases |

**Paid unevenly, and the order of magnitude matters more than the figure:** arbitration has two trials and the evening's largest samples; attention has a subgroup of 14 cases; skill has one unreplicated observation; signature has a share of documents, not an outcome.

**Who pays:** screening — in arbitration · diagnosis — in attention · documents — in signature · qualification — in skill. **Instrumental diagnostics pays none of the four:** its cost is 290 missed findings, a different plane entirely.

**All six distortions from the front door showed up:** anchoring and narrowing — in attention, decay — in skill, correction — in signature, disagreement — in arbitration; alert stream got no column of its own. **Exactly one is named by its own name over the evening.**

## Speaker notes

The four payments of the axis — attention, skill, signature, and arbitration — are collected here by name: under each one sits what it cost, how it was measured, and which section pays it. The vocabulary of forms tells you where the work moved; this ledger tells you what happened to the person doing it. The row order is the same one the addresses were announced in at the start: the promise and the report on it read in the same order.

Attention. Visual search narrows under a wrong suggestion: the scan area covered by eye fixations falls from just over eleven to nine and a half percent, and in human terms that reads as: where the suggestion stayed silent, missed cancers rose to sixty-one out of a hundred that existed, against twenty-nine. The denominator sits right next to the number: a subgroup of fourteen cases, and it's significant. The order of evidence is a lab reading with eye tracking, replicated in direction, though in a different specialty the reading came out reversed. Alongside it sits a second figure for the same address: a correct decision flips to an incorrect one under a colleague's advice, a systematic review. Both are about where the person's eyes go, not about what they think.

Skill. Technique drops in work done without the assistant after people get used to it: from two hundred eighty-four procedures with a finding per thousand down to two hundred twenty-four — minus sixty findings per thousand, among the same nineteen doctors, an observational study, not replicated once. This work doesn't prove causation, and the authors don't claim it does. It's the weakest row in the ledger, and at the same time the only measurement of skill decay in real clinical work — both halves go together.

Signature. Nine out of eleven machine-written discharge summaries carried at least one error, and ten out of nineteen large fabrications; measured by a blind comparison of one hundred hospitalizations, each document read by two people. What's costly in this row is the price of refusing to sign: a doctor who accepted a suggestion in favor of standard treatment is judged, in a case review, as more reasonable than one who rejected it — and that's the judgment not just of juries but of two hundred forty-eight German doctors, meaning the very side the procedure assigns to do the reviewing. For a non-standard recommendation, there's no difference.

Arbitration. Two centers in England's National Health Service, fifty thousand women: without the model, three point nine percent of readings in one center and eleven point three percent in the other went to arbitration; with the model as second reader, nine point four and thirteen point eight. Growth of nearly threefold in the first center and by a quarter in the second, and that spread is set by protocol: the first center sends only disagreements on recall to arbitration, the second sends every recall at all. A separate Norwegian measurement, on four hundred forty-one thousand two hundred seventy-six cases, counted this in seconds: one primary reading averages forty-eight seconds, resolving one disagreement takes two minutes twelve. One arbitration review, by time, equals about five ordinary readings — a direct answer to the evening's question: after the model, the person performs an operation that costs as much as five of their old ones.

On the unevenness of the bill. The ledger has to be read by its third column, not its second: a figure with no stated order of evidence misleads worse than no figure at all. Arbitration has two independent trials and the evening's biggest samples. Attention has a lab measurement on a subgroup of fourteen cases. Skill has one observational study nobody has repeated. Signature has a share of documents with an error, and what happened to the patients because of those errors, nobody checked. Four rows in one table, and the trust owed to them differs by orders of magnitude.

Who pays. Screening pays, and pays with the evening's best-evidenced number — arbitration. Instrumental diagnostics pays none of the four, and that isn't a gap in the ledger: its cost is measured, and it's large — two hundred ninety missed clinically significant findings across half a million scans — but it sits in the plane of the outcome, not the plane of the person deciding. The four axis addresses measure what happened to the decision-maker; a missed finding measures what happened to the patient. They cannot be put in the same column.

There are two planes missing from this foursome entirely, and both are worth naming so the ledger doesn't look complete where it isn't. The first is trust and consent: explanation raises agreement and doesn't raise decision quality, the visit record gets written faster while the proofreading stays. The second is the working time of a real person who wasn't on the schedule before the tool arrived: the alert stream is paid for in exactly that, not in attention, skill, signature, or arbitration.

And one line, without which the ledger would read as nothing but a list of losses. The difference in error structure between human and model produces a payoff for the pair — a pooled measurement across one hundred six studies, where the sign of the effect flips depending on which side is stronger on the task, and both intervals sit entirely on one side of zero. The row has direction, not magnitude: the share of overlapping errors hasn't been measured, and the figures that circulate for it don't hold up against their own primary source.

Significance figures not shown on the slide: the scanned area narrowed at p equals 0.004, and the drop in human terms at p equals 0.002. Both come from the lab eye-tracking measurement; the drop in detection share in the section on skill carries no stated significance in that work, and it should not be presented as if it had been measured.

And one gap that has to be named as a gap. There is no stage of patient consent in this lecture at all: the evening moved through the stages at which decisions are made about a person, and not one of them measured the person the decisions were about.

The thread closes with the same phrase the slide at the front door opened it with, because it hasn't changed over the evening — it has only gathered more grounds. Warning doesn't help: a warning is a message the person notes and then keeps doing what they were doing. What helps is action — considering the opposite before agreeing; arbitration that actually has time on the schedule; a procedure that measures the operator without the tool. Of these three, the lecture has measured the first, counted the second in minutes, and the third doesn't exist in a single jurisdiction.
