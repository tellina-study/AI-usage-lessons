## Speaker notes

A human stays in the decision loop for as long as communication delay and the speed of events actually allow it. Where they do not allow it, control is handed to automation in advance with the human's right to intervene — not replaced by it entirely, and not taken away from the human after the fact of an accident, but handed over by an engineering decision made before the event happened.

Further along the cycle — the same communication constraint, stretched to an interplanetary scale, where teleoperation stops being an option at all. This is the bridge to why Section 1 is necessary on Mars, not merely convenient: at a delay of several minutes, let alone 22 minutes at the far end of the range, no human on Earth can be "in the loop" in the sense the Dragon commander was in the loop at a distance of two hundred meters from the station.

For Dragon, the first selection condition passes partially, via the exception "AI enabled something that was impossible without it"; the second is unconditionally industrial; the third is column b, with the hours of manual preparation named without an exact number. For predictive latency compensation, the first condition is met directly; the second is unconditionally industrial; the third is column b, with an honest caveat: the effect is named as a number from one publication, not statistics across many trials. For Astrobee, the first and second are met without caveats; the third is column b, with neither side having an exact number. For Starlink telesurgery, the first condition fails literally — there is no trained model at all; this is exactly the case the course deliberately provides a separate genre for: a breakdown of a false match, where the content is the failure of the selection condition itself, not a win over it.

Further on, Section 3 moves away from autonomy of action itself to a different type of task — design and forecasting.
