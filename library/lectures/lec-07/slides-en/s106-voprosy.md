## Visible slide text
**Is there an independent source of truth besides the model's own explanation?** A different instrument, a different analysis, a different person, or the thing itself. A check against self-explanation is not a check.

**Has it been measured that they object — and that they object correctly?** Two countable rates. Neither is measured — "the person checks" is no different from "the person signs."

**Can they physically keep up?** Seconds for the decision against the volume to read. The median time checking a suggestion is the same **1.99 seconds** that came up on the evening's axis; what's accepted without a check gets corrected afterward in **53% of cases against 18**.

**If you were putting a model into your own loop tomorrow — which one check would you make mandatory before switching it on?**

## Speaker notes

The three lines on the slide are what the lecture leaves in your hands. Each one is checkable against a specific system, and each has already been applied today in a breakdown — here it's only given its own name.

The first check separates a check from its appearance of being one. If the only thing a person checks the model's output against is the model's own explanation, there's no check at all: an explanation buys agreement and does not buy quality, and that's been measured. A simplified explanation of a wrong recommendation dropped choice accuracy below what the same person gives with no model at all. An independent source of truth is a different instrument, a different analysis, a different person, or the thing itself; self-explanation isn't on that list.

The second check turns the word "control" into a magnitude. Any loop in which a person confirms the model's decision has two countable rates: how often they object, and how often they object correctly. If neither is measured, the claim "the person checks" is no different, in any observable way, from "the person signs." In the lecture's breakdowns, this distinction showed up twice. Where the suggestion arrived before the person's own conclusion, they objected less often and worse. Where the procedure was restructured so the person only looked at disagreements, objecting became a separate piece of work — and its volume turned out to be countable.

The third check is about physics, not diligence. A check has a duration, and it gets compared against the volume that has to be read for the check to actually happen. The measured median for checking a suggestion is about two seconds; a suggestion can't be read in that time, and what's accepted without a prior check gets corrected afterward in 53 percent of cases against 18. The measurement was taken outside medicine, on programmers with a code assistant, and the transfer is named as a transfer. From here follows a conclusion that carries over to any industry: the requirement "the person confirms" is only achievable when confirmation actually has time on the schedule. If it doesn't, the signature will be there and the reading will not, and that gets fixed by the procedure, not by attentiveness.

As a separate piece of evidence, from a different subject and a different denominator, alongside the third check sits a census of cleared devices: the capacity of a person to intervene has been checked at 56 of 717 radiology devices, as of June 2024. The same point from another angle — the rule requires the possibility of intervening, and does not require that anyone count whether intervention happened.

The question is put about the listener's own working loop, not about medicine: it requires no medical experience, and an automated element deciding something on a person's behalf exists today in almost every working loop. We wait for an answer out loud; if the room stays silent, the first check is named and a direct question is asked — who in the room has an independent source of truth in their loop, and for whom is the screen the only source there is.

In the photo, two people are reading a series on a workstation. The slide proves nothing and confirms not one number from the lecture: it sits here so the question gets asked to a scene of a person looking at a machine's output, not to an empty frame.

These three checks aren't about medicine, and they should be said exactly that way: any one of them applies to a loop at a bank, in manufacturing, in software development — anywhere a person reads a model's output and signs off on the result.

The first check is about an independent source of truth. The model's explanation is not a source of truth: it's produced by the same mechanism as the output itself, and is aligned with it by construction. An independent one is a different instrument, a different analysis, a different person, or the thing itself. A loop where the only way to check the output is the explanation attached to it is closed in on itself.

The second is about two countable rates. How often the person objects, and how often they object correctly. If neither is measured, then "the person checks" is no different, by any observable feature, from "the person signs," and there's nothing to argue about.

The third is about the physics of time. Seconds for the decision against the volume that needs reading. The median time checking a suggestion is about two seconds, and what's accepted without a prior check gets corrected afterward in 53 percent of cases against 18. If confirmation has no time on the schedule, there will be a signature and no reading, and no instruction will change that.

Ask the room the question and wait for an answer: which one check would you make mandatory before putting a model into your own loop. The answer comes within a few seconds, and it's almost always one of these three.
