## Speaker notes

Let's return to the start of the lecture.

A ladder of five levels. AI climbs from field to shelf and works differently on each rung. L-one — an open biological environment; AI works on narrow tasks — See & Spray, xarvio; breaks in attempts at generic advisory and in attempts to build a closed-loop — the collapse of vertical farming. L-two — semi-controlled; specialization beats universality — LaserWeeder, Saga UV-C, Tevel; the universal "autonomous tractor" goes bankrupt — Monarch, FarmWise. The architectural choice within the AI domain — CV versus sensor-fusion AI — is critical. L-three — semi-closed individual-level; AI works more reliably — SenseHub, CattleEye, DeLaval; limitations — physical configuration, Holstein bias, economics. L-four — controlled cargo flows + fast feedback in basis points; agentic AI leads — Cargill CMAX, Tract, Olam. Federal policy — a tail risk; AI-MRV without direct measurement — large-scale greenwashing. L-five — a fully digitized environment; AI works reliably for established players — Walmart, Tesco since 2017, X5 since 2020; a hybrid status at Magnit. L-five successes don't prove the readiness of the whole chain — it's the specifics of retail-AI, not agriculture-AI.

Remember Plenty Unlimited in Compton? The opening in May 2023 with the mayor's delegation, reporters, and the promise of four and a half million pounds of leafy greens per year. The closure in December 2024 — after nineteen months. Raised capital of about nine hundred forty million from SoftBank, Walmart, Bezos Expeditions. The company's valuation: one point nine billion in January 2022 → less than fifteen million in early 2025 — a collapse of about ninety-nine percent. We asked at the very start: what exactly did AI do in this failure — and in what sense did it fail to do it?

Now the answer. Plenty did not close because of bad AI. Plenty closed because of LED thermodynamics. The climate controller worked. Computer vision recognized the growth stages. The ML yield models were trained and updated. What did not work — the arithmetic of energy: LED lighting in California at prices of about twenty-five cents per kilowatt-hour ate the AI optimization. For every five, ten, twenty percent of efficiency saved through ML, there remained a base consumption two orders of magnitude higher than that of an open field with free sun. This is AP-one — the law of thermodynamics matters more than ML: the first of five anti-AI criteria we've arrived at along the full trajectory of the lecture.

And this works not only for Plenty. The same logic can be laid onto every rung of the ladder. Where AI works — See & Spray at L1, LaserWeeder at L2, SenseHub at L3, Cargill CMAX at L4, Walmart Eden at L5. Where it breaks — Plenty at L1, Monarch at L2, Cainthus tie-stall at L3, the USDA Climate-Smart cancellation at L4, GNSS jamming plus Melitopol plus the Starlink ban at the environmental level. The ladder is a map of engineering decisions, not an "AI revolution in agriculture". Each rung has its own working tools and its own failure modes; the engineer's task is to know which tool to apply on which rung, and where AI is structurally unsuitable.

The engineer holds this whole ladder in mind, choosing the right tool for each rung, and knowing where AI doesn't work and why. This is neither "optimism" nor "skepticism" toward AI in agriculture — it's a decision-making apparatus: for a concrete task, name the ladder rung, assess the environmental conditions, apply the corresponding one of the five anti-AI criteria, name the tool or the alternative.

And a bridge to the next lecture. In Lecture 11 we move to L-eleven — cyber-physical manufacturing. This is an environment closer to L4-L5 in level of control — a production line is more closed-loop than a field — and at the same time with physical contact of AI with the product, as in L2 robotics. It's a combination of two environmental characteristics, and it gives a different class of AI applications — predictive maintenance, robotic assembly, quality control — which we'll examine using the same "environment → task → class of AI → failures → alternatives" frame as in this lecture.
