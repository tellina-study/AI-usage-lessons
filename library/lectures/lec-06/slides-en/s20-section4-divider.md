## Speaker notes

The surrogate from the previous part guessed from examples, but examples trained on physically correct data — which is why, within its domain, it is reliable. This part is about a class built on a fundamentally different principle.

A generative model of geometry and a large language model are trained to reproduce the statistical regularities of a corpus — what a part usually looks like, how a paragraph of a specification usually sounds. Their math contains no equilibrium equation, no notion of a force path, stress, fatigue, or safety factor. A diffusion model, when generating a bracket, produces a shape statistically similar to the brackets in its training data; it does not compute whether the shape will hold the load, because it contains no quantity that could be computed. This is a direct consequence of the foundation set out at the start of the lecture: the model approximates what an answer looks like or sounds like, not how an object works.

From this follows the consequence that defines the whole part: what is dangerous is not the error of a generative model by itself — every tool makes errors — but the combination of a confident, expert tone with the absence of any internal check. A wrong answer sounds exactly as convincing as a correct one. This is precisely what makes the cost of a hallucination in design especially high, and it is what ties the failures of this part to historical catastrophes at the seams between systems — which we will reach at the part's climax, in the case of Mars Climate Orbiter.
