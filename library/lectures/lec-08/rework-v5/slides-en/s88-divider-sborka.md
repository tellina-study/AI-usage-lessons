## Visible slide text

# Section 6. Wrap-up

**Five sections went by modality and produced thirteen worked cases. Not one new one gets added here** — what gets added is something none of them had on its own: one sentence that can say all of it.

**Three addresses · four markets · four refusal criteria.** And, separately, where the industry is heading, by the structure of the market: it changes slower than version numbers.

## Speaker notes

The five previous sections were built the same way, and that was done for ease of reading. First a description of the stage of the craft in its own words and the industry's own terms, then the classic modes with their measured numbers, then the worked cases, then the named price of what moved to the right. That structure is good for learning and bad for drawing a conclusion. A reader who has gotten this far is holding thirteen worked cases and a few dozen numbers in their head — and does not have a single sentence that can say all of it at once.

Wrap-up produces that sentence, and it does exactly four things here.

First. It answers the question asked at the end of Entry: execution stopped being the scarce resource — what is scarce now, and in what units is it paid for. The answer is assembled at three addresses, and behind each one stands a worked case with measured classic numbers. Selection, rights, discernment. Each of the three is named by name, each has its own unit of account, and each is backed by a number that has already sounded in one of the five sections.

Second. It presents a measurement that four independent platforms arrived at independently of each other — in different countries, by different methods, across different modalities. A music platform, a game store, the audiobook market, and a Russian music service measured different things and arrived at the same figure. A coincidence of result shape across such a set is rare, and when it happens, it is more reliable than any conclusion drawn from individual cases.

Third. It collects the criteria by which an engineer refuses a generative model. All of them are derived from how the method is built, as worked out in the previous sections; general cautionary considerations played no part in deriving them, and that is exactly why not one of them will close with the next model version. This is the most applicable part of the evening: a criterion stated so it can be checked before work begins — in advance, while the refusal still costs nothing.

Fourth. It looks at where the industry itself is moving. With one restriction, and it needs to be said up front: what goes on the frame is the structure of the market. A list of tools does not. A list compiled in October 2026 will be stale by the time it is read; five days before the material was due, preview access to one of the leading video models was shut down — the second flagship taken offline in thirteen months. The structure of the market changes slower than version numbers, and a forecast drawn from it is more honest.

What is not in the section is also worth saying. There is not one new worked case, and that is a deliberate restriction. A section that introduces fresh material in its last minutes reads as the sixth one in a row and stops being a wrap-up: instead of tying a knot, it unspools one more thread. Everything that follows has already been shown — only one thing changes, which is that it now stands next to the rest.

And one last thing to keep in mind going into the section. Thirteen worked cases break down into three unequal parts, and by the end of the evening those parts will be given a number. In seven cases the win is measured against the classic mode — that is, both sides are measured in the same unit, and you can see what it cost without the model and what it costs with it. In three, the classic mode is named but has no number, and that is said out loud on the breakdown's own frame. In three, no classic mode exists at all, and that is a property of the task itself: work that has never been done has nothing to compare against.

One of the seven stands on the border, and there is no need to hide it — quite the opposite, name it. Dubbing: its first classic mode, a live voice actor at $50–450 per finished minute, is measured in exactly the unit in which synthesis is measured, and so the win there is honest; its second mode, subtitles, is named and has no per-minute rate of its own. By the form of the breakdown, this row falls under the column "second mode with no number"; by the question "what is the win measured against," it falls among the measured ones. Both statements are true at the same time, and that is exactly why the count is shown in full rather than handed over as a ready-made number. Wrap-up lays the ground for that count, and the number itself will sound at the closing.
