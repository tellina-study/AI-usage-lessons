## Visible slide text
**Human participation is required, and not because they're more accurate** — it's been measured that they often aren't. The question is what they do after the model, and who measured that.

**The lower the rung, the weaker the evidence.** The fourth rung, also the most common, is the one we know the least about: the signature is there, the reading is not.

**All four showed up over one case** — the woman called in by letter by her year of birth: **①** at screening, **②** on her scan, **③** on her preventive chest X-ray, **④** on her discharge.

**Three grounds, and they hold up differently.** *The right to sign:* a case review evaluates the person's actions, and there's no question of the model in that procedure at all. *Errs differently:* across 106 studies, the sign of the pair's payoff depends on which side is stronger — the ground has no magnitude. *Cannot be undone:* a surgery can't be undone after the fact, a year of follow-up can't be replayed.

**The rule is phrased as a capacity to intervene — presence does not satisfy it.** And not one of the four rungs has been measured through to what the decision did to the patient.

## Speaker notes

The four rungs of human participation, and the three grounds this participation rests on, are brought together here into one ledger — first by rung, then by ground. The line about human participation was spoken at the front door as the lecture's own obligation; here it comes back earned.

The woman with the letter, who closes out the ladder of rungs, first appeared at the start of the first section: she was called in on one criterion, her year of birth, none of it her own. From there she has stayed with us the whole evening: the arithmetic of accuracy on two groups of ten thousand was computed about her, the wall between two independent readers held up her program, the report on her scan and the outcome line that will go out to her by letter sit in the fourth section. Her thread closes on the thought that pulls the whole evening together: the four rungs of participation are four positions one decision about one person passes through as it moves along the stages — not four different loops in four hospitals. At screening, the person standing over her case read first. At diagnosis, the person read after a suggestion. On her preventive chest X-ray, the person would have seen only what the model flagged, and fifty-five scans out of a hundred are read by no one. At discharge, a signature under a machine-written draft. And nobody chose her rung on purpose: it came out the way it came out, from how each procedure happens to be built.

The third rung is more dangerous than it looks: whatever the model doesn't flag never reaches the person at all, and they can only learn about what was missed from another source — the next exam, a complaint, a review after the outcome. In Moscow's sorting of preventive chest scans, this has actually been counted: two hundred ninety clinically significant findings turned up only because, within the study, every "normal" result was re-read by people — and in routine work there will be no such layer. You can check your own third rung with one question to the loop: what happens to what the filter didn't show, and who's counting it.

The strength of evidence for the fourth rung. The median time spent on a suggestion before accepting it is about two seconds, and it was measured on programmers with a code assistant — that is, outside medicine; the transfer is named as a transfer and is not presented as a clinical measurement. What's accepted without a prior check gets corrected afterward in fifty-three percent of cases, against eighteen. The first rung, by comparison, rests on a randomized trial of a screening program with a hundred five thousand women. The order of evidence drops exactly as participation becomes more formal.

Why the fourth rung is the most common is explained by the price of refusing consent: a doctor who accepted a suggestion in favor of standard treatment is judged, in a case review, as more reasonable than one who rejected it — and that's the judgment not just of juries but of two hundred forty-eight German doctors, meaning the very side the procedure assigns to do the reviewing. For a non-standard recommendation, there's no difference.

From here follows a consequence worth naming before moving to the grounds. If the most common rung is the one where the signature is there and the reading is not, and if disagreeing with a suggestion costs more at review than agreeing with it does, then the formal requirement of human participation cancels itself out. It demands the capacity to object while simultaneously making objecting the most expensive move available. The fix is a change to how a case gets reviewed after the outcome: as long as the review rewards agreement, the loop will slide toward the fourth rung under any set of instructions, and training doesn't help here — a separate measurement showed it doesn't raise the rung. The fourth rung isn't anyone's fault: it follows from how the procedure is built, and it's fixed by the procedure. If confirmation has no time on the schedule, the signature will be there, and the reading will not.

Now the three grounds, and each is presented with its own ledger.

The right to sign is granted to the person. In a case review, the committee evaluates the person's actions, and there's no question of the model in that procedure at all. And it has been measured how rarely anyone checks whether the person is even capable of exercising that right: the capacity to intervene — that, not the mere presence of a person nearby — has been checked at 56 of 717 radiology devices, as of June 2024. And it is precisely the person who gets asked for it, which is not the same as saying "the person matters more."

Errs differently. This ground has no magnitude: the share of overlapping errors between human and model has not been measured, and the figures that circulate for it don't hold up against their own primary source. What holds the ground up is mechanism and structure: across 106 studies, the pair performs worse than the model alone where the model is stronger, and better than either side alone where the human is stronger, and both intervals sit entirely on one side of zero. Alongside it is a measured asymmetry in rejections: more accurate findings by the model were rejected more often than less accurate findings of the person's own. Together this confirms the direction — human and model errors fall on different cases — and does not confirm the magnitude. That's enough for the ground: it claims the errors diverge, not on how many cases.

Cannot be undone. A surgery can't be undone after the fact, a year of follow-up can't be replayed, procedures carried out by a hand that has already slipped have already been carried out. A device, meanwhile, does get recalled, and this has been counted: of 950 cleared devices with a model, 60 were recalled, with 182 recall events among them, and 43.4 percent of recalls fall in the device's first year; as of November 2024. Decisions made on the device during that year are not recalled along with it. The two denominators here, 717 and 950, come from two different studies at two different cutoff dates, which is why each figure carries its own date.

And about the rule itself: it is phrased as a capacity to intervene, not as presence, and it does not require watching every decision. A person's presence near the loop does not satisfy it — which is exactly why "we have a human in the loop" says nothing on its own, until a rung is named.

And one last thing — about the rungs, not about the whole lecture, and the difference here matters. About the evening as a whole, you cannot say "there is no patient outcome in any breakdown": the Swedish screening trial did reach the outcome, and so did the surgeons' breakdown. But not one of the four rungs of participation has been carried through to it. What's been measured is behavior — where the eye looks, what the person signs, how many findings they make without the assistant. What happened to the patient because of it, nobody checked. This isn't a reproach to the researchers — a measurement like that takes years — but the lecture holds itself to the same order-of-evidence standard it demands of vendors. That's why the woman with the letter leaves the evening without an outcome: we know who stood over her case at every stage, and we don't know how it all turned out for her.
