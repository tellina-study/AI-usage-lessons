## Speaker notes

Let's resolve the paradox from the start of the lecture in full. Assembly became nearly free — true. Almost no one extracts value from it — also true, but with a caveat: twenty-five percent of those who reached a pilot, not five percent of everyone. The resolution: when assembly is free, the scarce resource becomes not execution but judgment — the ability to tell real signal from noise and keep intent and accountability with the human. An engineer who can build anything instantly but cannot say whether it worked is more dangerous than before, not safer.

The classical discipline of every phase is not obsolete: it is the apparatus for telling signal from noise, brought into operational form. What changed: the cost of assembly, the speed of research, the speed of prototyping, the speed of observation. What stayed human: formulating intent, judging signal against noise, accountability for every answer the product gives, the decision "AI is not the right tool here," governance with an owner.

The practical apparatus is an eight-question checklist you run against any phase before making it AI-first: is the classic in place, is the trust worth the cost, is there an eval and a reference dataset, is there a guardrail metric, are we rolling out gradually with the ability to roll back, is there guaranteed escalation to a human, who is accountable for the outcome, is the data safe. If even one answer is "no" — that doesn't mean "don't use AI," it means "there is a structural gap here that needs closing before, not after."

This course doesn't teach "use AI more" and it doesn't teach "fear AI" — it teaches a well-founded "yes" and a well-founded "no." In the paired seminar you'll walk the same loop on a training AI product — from hypothesis through a reference dataset and three evaluations to a guardrail metric and a human-escalation point. Ready for questions.
