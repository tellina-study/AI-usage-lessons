# Repeating questions and Olga's knowledge base

## Assertion

Half the day's requests ask about the same thing — and Olga closes every one of them by hand, against the same knowledge base.

## Visual

> **The whole knowledge base — ten items:** payment, rescheduling a class, documents, a refund, a retake, a missed class, a certificate, transferring to another course, holidays, contact with the instructor.

1. Most are about things already in the knowledge base: Olga knows the answer by heart and types it again. The rest (the loyalty program, a referral discount, a demo class) go to sorting-out — her manager, the contract, the academic office.
2. Telling one from the other takes a second, and that step is written down nowhere.

> **What it costs the center.** A repeating question sits in the same queue as a hard one, taking time away from requests where the knowledge base doesn't help much.

> Olga: "Let the process answer on its own from the knowledge base, and for everything else write 'a human will get back to you' and call me."

## Speaker notes

This is the same training center and the same process we ended last session with. A request arrives through the form, the process parses it, writes a row, and notifies Olga about it. Everything after that is Olga's — twenty requests a day, and she's the one who answers them.

She answers from the knowledge base — these ten items, in full: payment, rescheduling a class, documents, a refund, a retake, a missed class, a certificate, transferring to another course, working on holidays, contact with the instructor. This isn't a rulebook someone wrote once — it's what she most often ends up repeating.

And she repeats it literally. Most of the day's requests ask about exactly this: how to reschedule a class if you're sick; when the refund for a cancelled course arrives; what documents are needed to enroll. Olga knows the answer to each of these questions by heart — and still types it out again every time, because there's no one else to answer for her.

The remaining questions are different. About the loyalty program, about a referral discount, about a free demo class. None of that is in the knowledge base, and these questions get closed by sorting it out: ask her manager, check the contract, check with the academic office.

Olga tells one kind from the other instantly, without thinking — all ten items are in her head, and she sees right away where a question lands inside an item and where it only looks like it does. Here's what matters: that step is written down nowhere. It exists in Olga's head — and nowhere else. Not in the form, not in the table, not in the process.

The moment the process starts answering, the center pays for it in a simple way: a repeating question sits in the same queue as a hard one and runs into the same person. Time spent on what's already written in her own knowledge base doesn't go to requests where the knowledge base doesn't help.

So Olga asks for one specific thing: let the process close on its own what the knowledge base covers, and for everything else honestly write "please wait, a human will get back to you." And she immediately names what worries her about this: she's the one accountable for the answer, and she'll only see it after it has already gone out to the requester.
