## Speaker notes

The task we're entering is built differently from reading a scan. The patient arrives on an emergency basis, and within the next few hours someone has to decide: take them to the operating room now, or not. The surgeon makes that decision, and makes it from what they can see — the patient's appearance, their age, what they've had before, labs drawn an hour ago. There's no time for a case conference, and often no second opinion either.

The cost of both errors is named here before any accuracy figure, and the two halves aren't comparable in how measurable they are — that has to be said before any number. Underestimating the risk means taking the patient to the operating room as a routine case: no ICU bed prepared, no transfusion on standby, no conversation with the family about what might come next. A complication in that case is counted on day thirty, and counted well: the national surgical quality database collects exactly these outcomes. Overestimating the risk means delaying the operation or not performing it at all — and the person dies from what wasn't done. This half is completely invisible in the thirty-day count: the patient wasn't operated on, there's nothing to compare, and there's no way to learn that the surgery would have saved them, because the attempt never happened. The half that has no number is named outright and isn't replaced by an estimate.

At this point there isn't one classical mode but two, and both are counted. The first — formal risk scales used long before any machine learning: a physiologic-status scale, an emergency-surgery scale, an open online calculator. All three are computed on the same validation sample as the model, by the same measure, and that's rare: usually a new system is compared against someone else's published number, or not compared at all. The three scales give between 0.8743 and 0.8975; the model, on the same sample, gives 0.9162. The gain — that is, 0.019 to 0.042 — is noticeable but not dramatic, and it has to be stated exactly that way, not as "the model beats the scales."

The second kind of classical mode is a surgeon's judgment with no scale at all, and this study is built as follows. Thirty emergency surgeons were randomized into two groups of fifteen: one group assesses risk from a written case description on their own, the other after consulting the POTTER calculator. One hundred fifty cases, outcomes tracked forward thirty days. None of them examined the patient in person: they worked from the description, and that limitation has to be stated out loud, not hidden. The calculator itself is a decision tree, not a neural network, trained on 382,960 cases; input is four to eleven answers about the patient, output is the probabilities of five outcomes over thirty days. This measurement has never been repeated.

A caveat about the samples is needed here, or the two rows of numbers will blur into one. The three scales and the model are computed on a 2014 validation sample — tens of thousands of cases from the same national database. The surgeons were measured on a different one hundred fifty cases, tracked forward thirty days. The two rows sit side by side as two classical modes for one task, and they don't add up into one ledger: comparing 0.9162 to 0.820 directly is not allowed.

The result on three of the five outcomes landed exactly as deployment promised: on mortality, on bleeding, and on pneumonia, surgeons with the suggestion became more accurate than surgeons without it. Most deployment reports stop right there, which is exactly why the other two are worth naming.

The fourth outcome — ventilator dependence. This is the spot where the model's lead over the human was the largest of the five: 0.928 against 0.833. The rule the lecture just assembled from a hundred six studies predicts a gain for the pair here, and there isn't one: 0.834 against 0.833 — that is, exactly zero. Dropping this row and keeping the four convenient ones would have turned this case study into a pure demonstration of the rule.

The fifth outcome — septic shock — and this is the lecture's single most important number. Here the human was more accurate than the model: 0.820 against 0.816. The difference is tiny, and what matters isn't its size but its direction — this is the one place in the task where the surgeon had their own advantage. The surgeon who saw the suggestion scored 0.712. They didn't just fall below the model — they fell below themselves, on the one outcome where they didn't need any help. The pair came out worse than both the model alone and the human alone, and that has to be stated exactly that way, not as "the pair didn't improve the result."

What shifted here, by the vocabulary. The eighth form: same performer, now worse. Its checks pass literally. Same place — the risk assessment happens in the same spot. Same workload — the same one hundred fifty cases. Same hand — the surgeon gives the assessment, and the order of work hasn't changed. What changed is the state of the hand: the surgeon stopped relying on their own advantage exactly where they had it. Sitting alongside this is the fifth form — part of the risk assessment moved from the surgeon's own judgment onto the screen — but the load-bearing one here is the eighth, because what's measured isn't where the work happens but the quality of what the hand finds.

Two caveats on this measurement: no one examined the patient — the surgeons worked from written case descriptions; and this measurement has never been repeated.
