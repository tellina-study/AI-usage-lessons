## Speaker notes

Here is the load-bearing idea of today's whole lecture, and it's worth holding onto through all six sections: a product is a feedback loop — discover, build, measure, learn, decide, discover again. This isn't one theory from one author — it's a shape that at least three people from completely different fields, with no contact with one another, arrived at independently.

Walter Shewhart and W. Edwards Deming, statisticians in quality control, formulated the PDCA cycle — plan, do, check, act — as a method for continuous improvement of a manufacturing process; postwar Japanese manufacturing quality grew on this cycle. John Boyd, a military fighter pilot, formulated the OODA cycle — observe, orient, decide, act — as a model of who wins an air battle: not the one with the more powerful plane, but the one who runs this cycle faster and more precisely. Eric Ries, an entrepreneur, formulated the Build-Measure-Learn cycle as a way for a startup not to spend months on a feature nobody wants.

There's also an important nuance of the same shape — single-loop versus double-loop. A single loop corrects an error relative to a fixed goal, like a thermostat. A double loop goes deeper: it periodically questions the goal itself. This directly foreshadows the failure we'll cover in section four: training a model through human feedback is a mechanized single loop, and reward-function hacking is exactly its failure mode. A statistician, a pilot, and an entrepreneur drew the same blueprint without coordinating — that's a compelling argument that the loop isn't a fashionable methodology but a structural property of a system that learns.
