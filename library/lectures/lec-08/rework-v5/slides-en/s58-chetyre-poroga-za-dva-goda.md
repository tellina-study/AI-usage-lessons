## Speaker notes

Four events across two years, and each is a threshold after which something stopped being an experiment.

First. The 2024 film "Here": de-aging Tom Hanks and Robin Wright was computed during the shoot, on a monitor, meaning the director and actor saw what they were filming for the first time. The whole film came in at a $45 million budget — less than just the increase in The Irishman's budget, from $125 to $159 million. The second half is mandatory: the film was savaged by critics, and part of the criticism was aimed exactly at the "plastic" faces. The technical leap happened; the artistic one didn't — and it must not be presented as a triumph. It's useful to hold both halves together: cheaper and faster doesn't mean better, and in this case the gap between the two claims is the clearest it gets.

Second. The series "The Eternaut," April 2025: a building collapsing in Buenos Aires — the first shot generated by a model to make it into a final cut of an original flagship series. The company's co-founder stated on an earnings call: done ten times faster than with classical effects tools. And this is worth stopping on: this multiple has no denominator. There's no absolute timeline — the classical mode is named only as "months," with no specific figure — and no cost is named by the company either. This is a textbook example of a multiple presented with no baseline, and that's exactly the role it plays on the slide — as a claim that cannot be checked. The rule to take away from it: on hearing "N times faster," ask faster than what, exactly, and how long that "what" used to take.

Third. "Watch the Skies," May 2025: the first feature film with visual dubbing. The Swedish actors re-recorded their own lines in English themselves, and the model rewrote the lip movement in frame to match the English articulation. The classical mode is named by both of its halves: either subtitles, with a corresponding drop in box office on English-language markets, or dubbing by other people's voices, with a noticeable lip-sync mismatch. The U.S. premiere was May 9, 2025, and the work was approved by the union — the very same one that struck against the use of models in 2023. What moved to the side is a question with no answer yet: what now counts as the original version of the film, and which version gets submitted to festivals.

Fourth. Super Bowl LX, February 9, 2026: the first predominantly generated national ad in the most expensive advertising broadcast on the planet — thirty seconds, a vodka brand, produced by Silverside AI. The industry estimate that "half or more of that broadcast's ads used generative tools at some stage" is an expert estimate, not a measurement, and it must be named as exactly that. What is measurable: twenty months passed between the first branded ad made on a generative model, in June 2024, and the Super Bowl's national ad.

A fifth case from the same series isn't on the slide, but it's worth naming, because it's about something different. In September 2024, a generative company signed its first deal with a Hollywood studio: the model is trained on the studio's own library; the vice chairman expects savings running into millions and millions of dollars, starting with storyboards, then moving to backgrounds and effects. By 2026, the studio has taken an equity stake in the developer and is planning a series built on its own rights. There's one substantive point here, and it's a double one: training on your own library is the only way to sidestep the dispute over data provenance, and at the same time it's a way to make the studio's style reproducible without its original authors.

What the four thresholds have in common. Not one of them is a demo: each is a shipped product with a date, a client, and an audience. That's exactly what sets them apart from the ads the news cycle measures progress by. And not one of them retired the previous way of working: on-set de-aging didn't retire post-production, visual dubbing didn't retire subtitles, the Super Bowl ad didn't retire shooting. A threshold is the moment a method becomes admissible, not the moment the previous method disappears.
