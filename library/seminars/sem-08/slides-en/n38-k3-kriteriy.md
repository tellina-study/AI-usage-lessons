# Criterion: when the decision must be code

## Assertion

The criterion carries over to any threshold-based task of your own, not just refunds.

## Visual

> **The criterion.** A decision must be code when it formalizes as a condition over an already-structured value. Better still — eliminate the need for extraction entirely, if the data source can be made structured from the start.

> **The axis row, closed.** A decision left to the model bypassed its own rule in a measured share of runs — nine out of a hundred and one, and exactly at the boundary of the condition. A decision handed to an algorithmic gate — never.

## Speaker notes

This is the criterion you can carry out of this particular case and into your own work, without having to remember a single number or a single node's name.

A decision must be written as code whenever it can be stated as a condition over a value that is already structured: a number greater than or smaller than some threshold, a date earlier or later than a given mark, a status that either is or is not on a list known in advance. If the condition can be written down on paper without a single word of ambiguity in it, then it has no business sitting in a piece of text that a model is left to understand and interpret on its own. Its proper place is in code, where a comparison simply has no room left for two different readings.

And there is an even stronger version of that same criterion, one step earlier in the chain: better still, if you can manage it, is to eliminate the very need to extract the value in the first place. If the data source itself can be made structured right from the start — the way this can be done, say, with a request form — the comparison becomes simple not because we somehow taught the code to compare numbers; it already knew perfectly well how to do that. It becomes simple because the very moment at which something has to be pulled out of free text stops existing at all.

Now, let's close out this session's axis as it applies specifically to the third case. The axis keeps asking the same question: where in the chain does the model make the decision, and where must that decision belong to code instead — and what goes quietly unnoticed whenever that boundary is never named out loud, explicitly. Here the answer turns out to be specific and carefully measured, not vague or general: a decision left in the model's hands ended up bypassing its own stated rule in a measured share of the runs — nine out of a hundred and one, on the extended set, and exactly at one single point, right at the boundary of the condition, not scattered randomly across the whole range. A decision handed instead to an algorithmic gate, on that very same test, scored zero out of a hundred and one — that is to say, never. The difference between "measurably rare" and "never" is the entire distance that separates a decision you choose to trust to the model from a decision you choose to trust to code, at least as it applies to threshold-based conditions of this kind.
