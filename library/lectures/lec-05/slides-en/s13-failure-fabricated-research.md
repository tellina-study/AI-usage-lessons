## Speaker notes

In 2025, Deloitte Australia delivered a report worth four hundred forty thousand Australian dollars to the Australian government, containing citations to nonexistent academic articles and a fabricated quote attributed to a real court ruling. The report was prepared using Azure OpenAI without human citation-level verification. After an independent researcher discovered the discrepancies, Deloitte partially refunded the money and publicly acknowledged using generative AI. A baseline for comparison: this is not an isolated case — a database records more than seven hundred twelve court rulings worldwide with AI-hallucinated citations, of which about ninety percent occurred in 2025 alone.

The mechanism, tied to the Discovery phase: the failure sits exactly at the desk-research step — a secondary-research draft was accepted as a finished, verified review rather than an unverified draft. This is a direct violation of the mandatory practice from the previous slide: links exist precisely so verification is possible — skipping that step turns a research assistant into a fabrication machine.

Connection to the course's meta-theme: this failure is structurally identical to a whole class of failures we'll return to in section six — plausible-sounding fabrication accepted as verified fact. The lesson: model output in the discovery phase is an unverified draft, not a source of facts; every citation needs independent verification before becoming the basis of a decision. The criterion: if the task is to deliver a final document to an external client, one-hundred-percent verification of every citation is mandatory, not sampling. The alternative: traditional library research through verified databases with manual verification of every quote.
