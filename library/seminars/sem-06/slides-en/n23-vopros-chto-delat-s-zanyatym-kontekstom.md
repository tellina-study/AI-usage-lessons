# Four servers, one was needed. What do we change?

## Assertion

Which of you has more than three servers connected in your working project? And how many of them did you reach for over the last working week? Name both numbers — and say what you put as the first step and what you add to it next.

## Visual

> Who has more than three servers connected in their working project? And how many did you reach for over the last working week?

> **Name both numbers — and say what you put as the first step and what you add to it next.**

`nothing — the context is large, it will do` · `disconnect the servers you do not use` · `leave them connected, but get the definitions on demand` · `keep a set of servers for the task, not a shared project one` · `compact the conversation more often`

## Speaker notes

This starts with two numbers of your own. How many servers are connected in your working project — and how many of them did you reach for over the last working week? Both numbers can be found out in a minute: the list of connected servers opens with one command, and the calls made over a week a person remembers themselves. The difference between those numbers is that part of the count the whole case is about, only your own rather than out of the scene.

The setting of the scene is presented in full on the two previous screens: four servers connected by the project file, two of them loading definitions forcibly, one of them needed over the task. The question introduces not one new circumstance and asks about two steps — what gets put first and what gets added to it next.

Five cards: nothing — the context is large, it will do; disconnect the servers you do not use; leave them connected, but get the definitions on demand; keep a set of servers for the task, not a shared project one; compact the conversation more often.

The two-step form of the question was chosen because the answer here is a sequence. The cards look like mutually exclusive options, and with a single choice the discussion degenerates into an argument about which of them is better.

The answers distribute predictably, and every distribution means something of its own.

More often than the rest people name "disconnect the surplus" and "get the definitions on demand" — roughly equally. That is a working division: both measures act, and they cost different amounts. One requires knowing in advance what will be needed in this session; the other requires nothing and is often already on by default. The breakdown shows that these are two steps of one sequence with different costs, and puts them in order.

"Compact the conversation more often" comes up almost always and is mistaken about the mechanics. Compaction works on the history of the conversation; tool definitions arrive anew in every new context, separately from the history, and they do not go away with compaction.

"Let the colleague remove his own servers" is a legitimate move and a fragile one. The colleague needs those servers exactly as much as the first person needs the tracker, and the project file is one for everybody. That move leads precisely to the fourth card: the set of servers gets divided up by scopes, and the argument about somebody else's lines becomes unnecessary.

"Nothing — the context is large" is picked rarely, and it is worth saying out loud: "nothing" is exactly what happens by default for everyone who has never looked at what their session's context is made up of before the first question. The option nobody picks turns out to be the most widespread in practice.

The count from the scene is useful to keep in mind throughout the breakdown: over the task the calls went to one server, and the cost was paid for four.

If your own two numbers were not to hand, they are worth collecting before the breakdown: the list of connected servers opens with one command, and the calls over a week are counted from memory. Until that difference is named in whole numbers, the case reads as somebody else's.
