## Speaker notes

The four measurements that came up in this section answer one and the same question, and they have to be assembled out loud, or they'll stay four different numbers in four different places. The question was asked earlier: can adding a human to a machine make the result worse, and does this happen with professional clinicians. The answer is yes, and it's been measured four times, from four different angles.

The first measurement is the strongest of the four by study design. Thirty emergency surgeons were randomized into groups, one hundred fifty cases were tracked forward thirty days, and both human groups and the model were measured on the same cases. On septic shock — the one outcome where the human was more accurate than the machine — the pair scored 0.712 against 0.816 for the model alone and 0.820 for the surgeon alone. This is a direct measurement of what was promised: the pair falls below each side on its own.

The second measurement is the trio the lecture opened with. Fifty practicing physicians, randomized into two human conditions: a physician with ordinary reference materials scored 74 out of a hundred, a physician on top of the model scored 76, and that difference isn't significant. The model-alone run, 92, wasn't an equal randomized arm and ranks lower in strength of evidence than the rest. The tasks here are constructed case descriptions, there's no physical exam, and no one checked patient outcomes. So this measurement is weaker than the first on two counts at once, and both have to be named.

The third measurement isn't a single study but a review of a hundred six. A preregistered review with a meta-analysis pulled together seventy-four studies, and the inclusion criterion decides everything there: a study was only included if all three quantities were measured — the human alone, the model alone, and the pair. Where the model is stronger than the human, the pair is worse than the model: an effect size of minus 0.54, with an interval from minus 0.71 to minus 0.37 — negative throughout — and on that same subset of studies the pair beats the human alone by 0.74, with an interval from 0.63 to 0.85. Both halves have to be named together, or the table row reads as a verdict. The headline figure for the whole review is minus 0.23, with an interval from minus 0.39 to minus 0.07, and it's against this figure that minus 0.54 reads as a subset of studies. The review is stricter than any single case by design, and broader in scope: medical studies make up only part of it, it doesn't touch patient outcomes, and the studies are of different kinds.

The fourth measurement answers not "how much" but "through what mechanism." Ten readers from a national screening program read the same set of sixty cases twice — first without suggestions, then six weeks later with them. On cases where the suggestion missed the finding, the same reader's sensitivity fell from 71 out of a hundred to 39. The comparison is a person against themselves, which removes the between-person variability that's large in reading scans. The weakness is named in the same breath: there are ten readers, and you can't generalize to the profession from ten.

Now the conclusion, one for all four. The human-and-model pair doesn't inherit the strength of the stronger side. That isn't the same as "the pair performs poorly": the pair usually beats the human without the model, and that has its own figure — an effect size of 0.64, with an interval from 0.53 to 0.74 across the whole review (0.74 on the subset where the model is stronger). The formulation the data supports reads: the pair is usually better than the human and worse than whichever of the two is stronger. From this comes a practical rule: before deciding who decides, you have to measure which of the two is stronger on this specific task.

And an honest caveat that applies to all four at once; it has to be formulated precisely. You cannot say "a patient outcome isn't measured in any of the four": for the surgeons, a primary outcome is stated outright — one hundred fifty patients, thirty days of prospective follow-up — and the surgeons' own slide says that a complication is counted on day thirty. This was checked against the stated primary outcome of each of the four studies, the same way any borrowed figure gets checked.

The correct formulation holds, and the point stays the same: in none of the four did the human's decision actually reach a patient. For the surgeons, the case descriptions are built from admissions that had already happened, the participants' assessments never fed into treatment, and the outcomes serve as the reference standard against which the accuracy of those assessments is scored. For the physicians with the language model, there are no patients in the study at all — the tasks are constructed. The review, by its very subject, isn't about outcomes. The eye-tracking study measured accuracy, reading time, and gaze behavior. In other words, what's measured is how a person looks and how they trade a correct decision for an incorrect one — and what's not measured is what happened to the patient because the person looked worse. The difference between "the outcome isn't measured" and "the decision never reached a patient" isn't nitpicking: the first is false, and demonstrably false; the second is true, and is in fact the conclusion itself.
