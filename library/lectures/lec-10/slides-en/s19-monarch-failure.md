## Speaker notes

Let's move to the canonical L2 failure of 2025 — Monarch Tractor. This is a California company founded by Tesla alumni and Karlo Mondavi in 2018. The idea looked convincing: an electric tractor with an autonomous mode for vineyards and vegetable farms. Raised capital — more than two hundred twenty million dollars, of which one hundred thirty-three million in the 2024 round. The contract manufacturer — Foxconn since 2022.

The failure-case timeline is useful to see in full, because this is a "demo ≠ production deployment" pattern. In 2018-2020 the R&D phase, prototypes. In 2021-2022 — the commercial launch of the MK-V model; a price of about eighty to one hundred thousand dollars per unit; first deliveries to California vineyards. In 2022 — the Foxconn investment, which reinforced the marketing claim. In 2023-2024 — claims of "autonomous fleet operation"; the peak of capital raising in 2024.

And then — the cascade of events of 2025-2026. August 2025 — Monarch loses Foxconn: Foxconn sold the Lordstown plant to Crescent Dune LLC for three hundred seventy-five million on August fourth, 2025. This is the first clear signal of decline — Foxconn would not have withdrawn the investment and exited contract manufacturing without structural problems.

September 2025 — a lawsuit by Burks Tractor Co. in an Idaho state court. Ten tractors of 2024 build for seven hundred seventy-three thousand eighty-eight dollars, which were "unable to operate autonomously" — the promised functionality didn't work. This is the trigger of a legal cascade of liability: the dealer files suit against the manufacturer, fundamentally — a mismatch between "autonomous" marketing and contractual reality.

On November nineteenth, 2025, Monarch sent out a corporate letter about layoffs of up to one hundred two people, about thirty-eight percent of staff, with a warning of possible shutdown. The company claimed it was transitioning from an OEM role into a SaaS provider of autonomy for existing third-party tractors — but "the timing of the transition puts Monarch at risk of shut down" in its own corporate memo.

And the final point — April fifteenth, 2026. Caterpillar acquired Monarch Tractor. This is a post-failure acqui-hire: IP plus the engineering team consolidated into Cat's autonomous mining and construction division. The Monarch brand effectively gone.

What structural failure? Several are at work simultaneously here. The first — "autonomous" marketing without disclosing supervised autonomy. The marketing sold "autonomous", but the real machines required operator supervision; demo conditions are not equal to reliability in production deployment. The second — sub-optimal economics for a general-purpose orchard tractor. The third — specialization wins. In those same 2024-2026 years LaserWeeder G2, Saga UV-C, Tortuga — all in commercial deployment. Narrow specialized solutions survive; universal "autonomous farm robots" go bankrupt.

The main lesson learned — an anti-AI criterion echoing AP-four: demo does not equal production deployment. Marketing a product as "autonomous" when the autonomy won't survive judicial scrutiny is a structural trap for the whole category. AI autonomy in agriculture is not yes/no, but a gradient with dozens of edge cases. The alternative: supervised autonomy with explicit disclosure of capability and non-capability for each claim.

And a final observation about the Caterpillar acquisition. This is not a revival of the technology; it's a closed door for Monarch's direct-to-consumer model and an open door for the retrofit mode — the AGCO PTx Outrun from the previous slide — on mixed equipment fleets. A strategic acquirer bought the R&D groundwork cheaper than it would have built it itself. This is the final state of failed bets, not their rebirth.
