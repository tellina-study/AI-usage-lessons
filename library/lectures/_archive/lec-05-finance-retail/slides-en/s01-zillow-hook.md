## Speaker notes

Let's start not with excitement and not with a warning, but with a single date and a single number. In November 2021, Zillow — the largest real-estate listings platform in the United States — shut down an entire line of business, Zillow Offers. This was algorithmic iBuying: a computer model forecast the future price of a specific home, and based on that forecast Zillow automatically bought the home, did cosmetic repairs, and resold it. The idea is elegant: remove the human appraiser, scale up home buying to thousands of homes in dozens of cities at once. In practice the model systematically overvalued the properties; the company bought homes for more than it could sell them for.

The outcome was severe: an inventory write-down of more than three hundred million dollars in a single quarter, cumulative losses for the line of business estimated at half a billion and more, layoffs of roughly a quarter of the workforce — about two thousand people, and a stock drop of about a quarter in a matter of days. An entire line of business at a large public company was shut down because of the errors of a single predictive model.

This is worth pausing on. This was not ChatGPT and not "artificial intelligence that can do everything" — it was a predictive model estimating a numeric value from tabular and geographic data. A large language model was not used here and could not be used: the task is not a text task. And the forecast error itself is a normal phenomenon — every model makes errors. The business collapsed not because of the fact of the error, but because of where that error was wired in: to an automatic, irreversible, capital-intensive action at the scale of thousands of homes, with no human in the loop. Think for thirty seconds: what type of AI was this — and why did an ordinary error become a business loss? We will return to the full analysis in the first section.
