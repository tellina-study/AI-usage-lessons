# Arup CFO deepfake scam — $25.6M (Case 6)

## Assertion

Arup CFO deepfake scam: Hong Kong Jan 2024, $25.6M across 15 transactions. A finance worker on a video call with a deepfake CFO + colleagues.

## Speaker notes

The sixth case — the Arup CFO deepfake scam. January 2024, Hong Kong. Arup — a large British engineering firm, known as the designer of the Sydney Opera House and many other landmark structures. The attack scenario. An Arup finance worker in Hong Kong received an email from a person claiming to be the company's CFO, with an invitation to a video call to discuss a confidential transaction. The finance worker accepted the invitation. On the video call were the "CFO" plus five-to-six other company employees, with recognizable faces and voices. All were deepfakes. On the call the "CFO" gave instructions to make fifteen transactions totaling two hundred million Hong Kong dollars — twenty-five point six million US dollars. The finance worker, following the instructions of the person he recognized as his CFO on the video call, carried out all fifteen transactions. The money vanished. Arup discovered the scam only when the finance worker asked the CFO a routine question directly through another channel. CNN and Fortune published detailed coverage of the case. This is the first publicly documented case of corporate fraud of this scale via deepfake video, and it set a new baseline of corporate security risk. What this incident means practically. A video call has ceased to be identity verification. In 2024, real-time deepfake-video technology became commercially available and cheap. One scammer with access to public photos and videos of target persons can stage a video call with realistic deepfakes of eleven-to-twelve people at once. Lesson for the engineer: a video call is not equal to identity verification in 2024 and later. Financial transactions require verification through an independent channel — a callback to a known number, multi-factor authentication, ideally face-to-face confirmation for large sums. This case is a wake-up call for financial processes inside companies. If in your company transaction authorization is based only on recognition of "the CFO's face" via a video call — you have Arup-class risk, and it must be closed structurally: procedurally via a mandatory callback, technologically via cryptographic signatures or MFA on transactions.
