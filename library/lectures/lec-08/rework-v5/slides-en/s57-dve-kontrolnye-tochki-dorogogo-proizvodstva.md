## Speaker notes

Two points that set the scale for the whole section: one shows how cheap an intermediate operation has become, the other how much expensive production cost right before the generative wave.

The first. The effects for "Everything Everywhere All at Once," winner of the Best Picture Oscar, were made by an independent five-person studio: they closed out more than 80 percent of about 500 effects shots. VFX artist Evan Halleck applied a background-removal tool to the "rock universe" scenes, where characters had to be cut out of the frame frame by frame. He described the classical mode as a very slow and painstaking process and named the baseline — half a day per shot; with the model, he says, he cuts characters out and drops them cleanly onto a backing plate in minutes.

There's nothing to argue about in this case, and its value lies exactly in having a baseline. Both sides are named: half a day and minutes. The multiple is computable from them, and it's huge — but measured against a real mode, not against thin air. A second detail: this is an intermediate step, the result was finished by hand, and nothing notable moved off to the side — no authorship dispute, no legal question, no job loss inside the studio itself. Five people got to take on a job that used to require a large team, and they took it.

The second point is The Irishman, 2019. The budget grew from a stated $125 million to $159 million, and the growth is attributed specifically to the digital de-aging of three actors: about 1,750 shots, the largest use of the technique in a feature film at the time; preparation at the effects studio started in 2015, four years before release.

Everything here rests on the VFX supervisor's caveat, and it must not be lost: per shot, it costs the same as in an ordinary effects film, and it comes out expensive because there are enough shots here for two films. This is the correct unit of account. Without it, the $159 million reads as the price of the technology; with it, it's clear that the price per shot was ordinary, and what was unusual was the volume. The exact same unit-swap turns up in all three breakdowns of the section, and it's useful to see it first on classical material, where there's no model at all yet.

Why hold both points side by side. Between them lie five years and two opposite stories about the same industry: in one, technology let five people do the work of a large team; in the other, it added thirty-four million to the budget. Both are true, and both are about effects. The difference is in what the technology was spent on: in the first case, cutting down routine work; in the second, scaling up a complex job. This is the general law of the whole sixty-year chain of cost reduction the lecture has been tracing from the start: the volume produced grows faster than the unit price falls, and savings on an operation do not automatically turn into savings on a project.

The section's numbers should be read against these two scales. A two-thousand-dollar ad is cheap next to the classical budget's forty thousand, and tiny next to a hundred fifty-nine million. Seventy thousand generated clips is a huge volume of attempts and, at the same time, seventeen-to-forty-two thousand dollars of compute — a fraction of a percent of a major campaign's budget. Without the two anchor points, both numbers read as sensational; with them, as a relocation of the expensive point inside the chain.

The first case is also notable for the praise given to the tool itself. The artist doesn't say "the tool did the work for me"; he describes one specific operation — cutting a character out of a frame and placing it on a backing plate — and names both sides by time, before and after. This is a model formulation of a result, and it's worth demanding from any adoption report: name the operation, name both time figures, name what's left to the person. A report that has only the multiple in it doesn't pass this test.

The second case teaches the opposite lesson. There, too, is a number, and a huge one, but the size of a budget by itself says nothing about the technology or its efficiency until the unit is named. The correct unit, named by the supervisor, flips the reading: the price per shot is ordinary, the volume is unusual. Without that line, The Irishman reads as proof that digital de-aging is ruinously expensive; with it, as proof that it's the volume the director chose for artistic reasons that is ruinous.

From here comes a general method for reading other people's numbers, one that works across all three modalities of the lecture. Any large number in production breaks down into a unit price and a unit count. Until it's broken down, it's unclear what it's even evidence of. Four hundred thousand generations — is that a lot of attempts or an expensive attempt? A hundred fifty-nine million — is that an expensive technology or a lot of shots? Seventy thousand clips — an expensive campaign, or a cheap attempt repeated seventy thousand times? The answer in every case comes only from the breakdown, and in two of the three cases it has already been done for us by the people who did the work.
