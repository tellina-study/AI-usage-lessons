## Speaker notes

The ninth case — Sports Illustrated fake authors plus Amazon Kindle fake books. November 2023. Futurism, a technology magazine, published an investigative exposé: Sports Illustrated — one of the most famous American sports magazines — published articles under fake author names with AI-generated profile photos. For example, "Drew Ortiz," presented as an experienced sports writer with a photo of an ordinary American reporter, was entirely fictional. The photo was generated via AI services that sell face photos; the articles were, at least in part, AI-generated. The investigation then led to a public scandal and to a change of leadership at the SI publisher. A parallel pattern on Amazon Kindle. The Authors Guild over 2023-2024 recorded a surge of fake books on Amazon Kindle with AI pseudonyms imitating real jazz musicians, financial advisors, and other profession figures. Concrete documented cases — Frank Gioia, Ted Alkyer — fake author names passing themselves off as real jazz personalities. What these cases demonstrate structurally. Accumulated trust is a key brand asset for publishers. Sports Illustrated had many years of a reputation for quality sports journalism. AI pseudonyms in one investigative cycle destroyed this reputation — powerfully, visibly, and in the media. Similarly — Amazon Kindle ranking systems turned out to be easily exploitable by AI clones, which undermined trust in the Kindle bestseller list as a marker of quality. Lesson for the engineer: accumulated trust equals a key brand asset. AI pseudonyms destroy it instantly. If you publish under a name — the name must be a real person, or explicitly disclosed as AI-authored. This is not just an ethical principle — it is a competitive necessity for publishers who want to maintain the trust of their audience. If you build a content platform with AI-generated content — you must have a transparent disclosure layer. If you do not mark which content is AI-generated — you have SI-class risk, and its outcome is a public exposé plus lost audience trust. At the product level, this means — you must have UI patterns that explicitly show AI-generated content, and editorial policies that prohibit AI pseudonyms.
