# Visible content

## Title bar
Searching accumulated support tickets for the pain

## Body
[what it is → how it works → with AI and without → where it breaks]

**WHAT IT IS**

If a product already has users, it also has an archive of their complaints and questions. The practice turns that archive into a source of hypotheses: the tickets go into a searchable store, the ones close in meaning are gathered into groups, and you look at which themes repeat. What leaves is a named theme of pain.

**HOW IT WORKS**

1. Every ticket goes into one common store, tagged with when it arrived, who it came from and which product it is about.
2. The search runs **two ways at once**: on an exact match of words, and on meaning. An error code is found only by the first; a problem described in a person's own words only by the second.
3. What is close is gathered into groups and retold in two passes: first each ticket on its own, then what the group has in common. Skip the first pass and 20–40% of the detail is lost.

[Labelled diagram on the right: store and tag → search by word and by meaning → gather what is close into groups; caption "the order of the steps: skip the tagging and the archive becomes a bag of text"]

**WITH AI AND WITHOUT**

| WITHOUT AI — tagging by theme, by hand | WITH AI — what changed |
|---|---|
| Tickets are sorted into a list of themes thought up in advance. So the only themes found are the ones somebody has already guessed at. | What is close in meaning gathers itself, with no list of themes, and a group is retold in minutes. **The name of the theme is still checked by a person:** the machine groups, it takes no decisions. |

[Gold callout — WHERE IT BREAKS]
The archive is biased **by construction**: it holds only the people who got as far as complaining. Volume does not cure that — **there is no such ticket as "I need the thing you do not have at all"**. The question "why do people not get as far as signing up" is not one this practice answers: that takes a conversation with the people who left. And the search returns the tickets that are **close**, it does not measure importance: ten thousand complaints about one typo are still one typo.

## Speaker notes

The second practice works where a product already has living users and an accumulated archive of tickets.

What it is, in plain words. Any working product has a history of complaints and questions, and usually that history lies there as dead weight: people look into it when they need to answer one particular person, and nobody looks at it as a source of knowledge about the product. This practice turns the archive into a source of hypotheses. What leaves is a named theme of pain, propped up by verbatim quotes, and an indication of exactly who it is worth talking to in person about it. A general feeling that users are unhappy about something is not an output here.

Step one — store and tag. Every ticket goes into one common searchable store, tagged with when it arrived, who it came from and which product it is about. The tagging is not bureaucracy: without it the archive turns into a bag of text, in which last year's problem cannot be told apart from today's, or your own segment from somebody else's.

Step two — search two ways at once: by words and by meaning. An exact error code is found only by the word search, and a problem described in a person's own words only by the meaning search, and tickets contain both. On its own, each way misses quietly.

Step three — gather what is close into groups and retell each group in two passes: first each ticket separately, then what they have in common. Skipping the first pass has a measurable effect: the retelling loses between twenty and forty per cent of the important detail.

And what changed here. People have always been able to go through tickets, but they sorted them into a list of themes thought up in advance — and found only what somebody had already guessed at. A model gathers what is close in meaning on its own, with no list of themes; the name of the theme is still checked by a person.

The boundary has two parts, and both are compulsory. The first: the archive is biased by construction — it holds only the people who got as far as complaining. Volume does not cure it, a hundred thousand tickets are biased exactly as a thousand are, because there is no such ticket as "I need the thing you do not have at all". The archive is structurally silent about an unmet need, so the question "what do we not have" is not closed by it. The second: the search returns the tickets that are close, it does not measure importance. Ten thousand complaints about one typo are still one typo. Frequency is not significance, and the two are especially easy to confuse when the number is large.
