# The task arrived as one sentence. What do we speed it up with?

## Assertion

One sentence, one deadline. There is more than one means of speeding it up, and the choice between them is made by the task itself.

## Visual

## Speaker notes

Next — a practical exercise. The task will be a real one, with one deadline and no clarifications, and laying it out will be up to you: first into pieces, then by the dependencies between them. The decision about what to speed it up with is also left to you — on the next two screens it gets taken, and only after that is it tested.

The form the task is put in is ordinary to the point of dullness: one sentence and one deadline. No clarifications will follow, nobody attached a breakdown into stages or set priorities inside the task. That is the form in which work arrives most often, and laying it out falls to whoever is doing it: the person who set the task will not do that part for them.

There is more than one means of speeding up a task like this. One of them is what this rung is about: a separate worker the agent calls in for a piece of the work. The other four are older than it and on many tasks cheaper. The whole case is about how a choice is made among five means, and the choice here is made by the task itself. Its choice can only be seen once it has been laid out.

From that it is clear why the layout comes first — a question that occurs to everyone in a hurry to start. A task taken whole admits one order of work: from beginning to end, piece after piece, in whatever order they come to hand. Laid out, it admits several orders, and among them there are noticeably shorter ones. Until the pieces are named and the dependencies between them checked, the short order is not visible at all — there is nothing to see it with.

The decision point is posed as a question deliberately. Almost everyone has an answer to it, and it is half-correct: speeding it up will work, and the means will come from a different direction than expected. A ready-made statement in this spot would take away from the exercise the work it exists for: once you have read the conclusion, you cannot lay the task out with your own hands any more — all that is left is to agree with somebody else's layout. So here there is a question, and the answer to it is assembled as we go.

The previous case of this rung answered a different question: what to do when the reading needed for one answer is so much that it does not fit into the session's context. The answer there was a separate worker who reads in its own context and comes back with a short result. Here the question sounds different: there is one task, and its deadline is Monday. The two cases have one thing in common — a separate worker as one of the possible moves; the reasons for setting one up are different, and the boundaries of those reasons are different too. What rescues you on a long read starts getting in the way on short dependent work, and where exactly will become visible further on.

The name of the means is not on the divider. A divider names the observable problem and leaves the decision to the case itself — the same convention holds for the seminar's other five dividers. If the word "subagent" came to mind before you reached the cards, there is nothing to correct: it is one card out of five, and we will get to it.
