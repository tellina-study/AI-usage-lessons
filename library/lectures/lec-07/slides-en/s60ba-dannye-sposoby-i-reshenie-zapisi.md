## Visible slide text

**The data for this stage is everything gathered earlier, put into text.** The stage adds no measurement of its own.

**Five ways a note gets produced:** typed by hand with the patient present · typed after the visit · dictated · auto-populated from the record's own fields · carried forward from a previous note.

**The decision this stage makes is what goes into the document that treatment will later be ordered from.** It comes after observation: the record gets read later, and a different person reads it, carrying their own question.

## Speaker notes

Keeping medical documentation is a separate piece of a physician's work, with its own form, its own deadlines, and its own accountability for content. The stage adds no measurement of its own: it puts into text everything the preceding stages gathered, and decides what goes into the document a decision will later be made from. The previous stage left behind a trail of measurements, alerts, and what was done about them; from here on, the document does the work. A completed record serves three purposes at once: a source of information for the next physician, the subject of a quality-of-care review, and the primary evidence when a case gets reviewed.

The data for this stage is everything the earlier stages gathered, put into text. The complaints and history from the visit, exam findings, test figures, the diagnosis as formulated, the treatment plan, the trail of repeated measurements. The stage adds not a single measurement of its own; it converts what's been gathered into a form that can be handed off.

There's one question being settled with this data, and it isn't about archiving: what does the next reader, who wasn't at the visit, need to learn. The record isn't made for storage and isn't made for an inspector — it's made for a person who will act on it without having seen the patient.

Now about the ways a note actually gets produced, and this is worth pausing on, because it's built the opposite way from how the stage is usually pictured. Five ways are listed on the slide; I'll go through each. In a modern record they all work together, inside one document. This list is about where the characters in a note come from; the modes of writing that come later in the section are about who puts the text into the document. Two different axes. Typing by hand during the visit — the physician types while talking with the patient. Typing after the visit — between patients, or at the end of the day, that same take-home work with the chart. Dictation — speech transcribed into text, either by a person or by speech recognition. Auto-population — content the system pulls into the note on its own, from structured fields: the medication list, lab results, monitoring readings. And carrying forward a previous note — an earlier note moves into the new one, whole or in blocks, with edits for what's changed.

The proportion between them was measured keystroke by keystroke, and it's on screen as plates. The electronic record system at a large medical center tracks the origin of every character in a signed note: typed by hand, auto-populated by the system, or carried forward. Across twenty-three and a half thousand notes from four hundred sixty authors, it came out like this: eighteen percent of characters typed by hand, forty-six carried forward, thirty-six auto-populated by the system. Among residents, the share typed by hand is even lower, and the carried-forward share higher — eleven and eight tenths against fifty-one and four tenths.

The unit here is characters, and that has to be held firmly: later on, a carry-forward share of fifty and one tenth percent comes up, and that's a share of WORDS, from a different study with a different sample. Forty-six and fifty-one aren't the same number measured twice.

This proportion has to be read plainly: the physician types a smaller part of the note by hand. Four characters out of five come from previous text or from the record's own fields. And from this comes the first thing to know about the ceiling of any tool that promises to take typing off someone's hands: it's going after exactly that eighteen percent. The strength of this evidence: a single-center cross-sectional measurement from the record system's own log — a behavioral measurement captured by the machine itself — and generalizing it to other institutions or other record systems hasn't been checked.

The document forms at this stage differ, and the work inside them isn't the same: a visit note in the chart, a progress note in the hospital, a discharge summary — the final document on the hospitalization, which the next physician then works from — and a referral to a specialist. Method and form are different things: the same form can be typed by hand, dictated, or carried forward.

Now the readers, and I'll name them out loud: there's no room for them on the slide. The record has five, and each one acts on it. The local physician, a month later: the record tells them what's already been checked, what's been ruled out, and what treatment was given. The next hospital, on readmission: the discharge document is the main source of information about last time. Another specialist at the same institution the patient was referred to. A review committee, when a case gets examined: it reads the record and decides whether the physician's actions matched approved protocols, and the record itself is the evidence. And the patient themselves, who's entitled to a copy.

From this comes a property of the stage that sets the cost of error: whoever decides based on the record never saw the patient. A physician reading someone else's note can't tell "this didn't happen" apart from "this wasn't recorded." A gap in the record and the absence of an event look identical to them, and they have nothing to tell the two apart with.

The decision this stage makes sounds simple and carries a lot of weight: what goes into the document a decision will later be made from. The document triggers actions — treatment gets ordered and cancelled based on the note, admission at the next institution is based on the summary, a place in the queue is based on the referral, and a review assesses compliance with protocol based on the contents of the chart.

And why this stage comes right after observation. The direct answer: the record gets read after it's made. But the second half matters more — it's read by a different person, carrying their own question. Observation decided "intervene now, or wait for rounds" from a trail it kept itself. Here, the decision is made by someone who has no trail — only text — and the quality of that text, for them, isn't a matter of style; it's the only channel through which someone else's decision reaches them.
