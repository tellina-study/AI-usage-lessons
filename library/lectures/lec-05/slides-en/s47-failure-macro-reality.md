# Visible content

## Title bar
60% explored → 20% piloted → 5% succeeded: that's 25% success among those who got there, not "95% failure"

## Body
[Funnel 60→20→5 vs the crossed-out headline "95% FAILURE"]

**4 questions to ask of any loud AI-failure number**
1. What is the denominator?
2. What counts as "failure"?
3. What is the author's conflict of interest?
4. Does it trace back to a primary source with a methodology?

[Gold callout]
BCG: 60% of companies track **not a single** financial KPI tied to AI value

## Speaker notes

Let's return to the report the lecture opened with. MIT Project NANDA, widely reported as "ninety-five percent of organizations get zero return." The real funnel of the custom track inside the report itself: sixty percent of organizations explored, twenty percent launched a pilot, five percent successfully deployed. The success rate among those who reached a pilot is five out of twenty — twenty-five percent — not "ninety-five percent of pilots failed." Separately: an undisclosed conflict of interest — all four authors build or sell agentic AI products through that same project.

Let's extract a transferable skill through four questions. First: what is the denominator — ninety-five percent of all organizations, including ones that never seriously piloted anything. Second: what counts as failure — "didn't pay off on schedule," "abandoned," and "works but delivers no value" are three different events. Third: what is the author's conflict of interest. Fourth: does the number trace back to a primary source with a methodology.

A meta-lesson about RAND: the widely repeated "precise" figure of eighty point three percent does not appear in RAND's own report — this is a secondary fabrication of precision, structurally identical to the Deloitte failure from section one. Triangulation: Gartner — only twenty-eight percent of AI use cases are fully successful; S&P — forty-two percent of companies plan to abandon initiatives, up from seventeen percent a year earlier; BCG — sixty percent of companies track not a single financial KPI. All sources point to the same root: not "the model is bad," but "there was no financial criterion agreed in advance." Lesson: critically check the denominator before using a number as an argument in a decision.
