## Speaker notes

Let us fold the lecture into one thought. We opened with a paradox: building is almost free, and few extract value from it. We went through six phases and on every one found the same thing in a new wrapper — artificial intelligence changes the cost of each step, and the trust you can place in it, by different amounts, and it leaves the order of the steps alone. When building fell to almost nothing, the scarce resource stopped being the ability to build and became the ability to tell real signal from noise, and to keep intent and accountability with a human.

Look at the photograph. This is an engineer at a computer-aided design workstation, the late seventies, a light pen instead of a mouse. That workstation is gone, that pen is gone, the company itself is gone. And the discipline of design he was practising is still here — and you will be practising it. That is the answer to the question of what in this lecture is worth remembering. Half the list of products will have changed by next semester; the way of reasoning will remain.

The way goes like this. Break the activity into phases with their inputs and outputs. On each one, ask what exactly got cheaper and what did not. For any measurable claim, demand a basis of comparison; an impression does not count. And apply the three axes — reversibility, the cost of an error, observability — to assign the phase its operating mode. Lecture four said the same thing about code; here we have done it for a whole product.

Seminar seven is where you apply all of this. You will walk the same loop on a practice product, from the hypothesis to the point where the answer goes to a human, and for each phase you will name the row of the table and the position on the three axes.

Lecture six carries the method into engineering design. There will be a loop of phases of its own there, and failures of its own, but the central question stays the same: what got cheaper, what stayed human judgment, and where the autonomy a system claims does not survive a check against the denominator. The difference is that an error there sets in metal, and it costs more than an error on a screen.
