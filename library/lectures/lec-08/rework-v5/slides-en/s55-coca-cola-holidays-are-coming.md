## Visible slide text


## Speaker notes

The most complete publicly named set of numbers for generative ad production — and the one case where the room's expectation diverges from the result so sharply that a prediction is built on it. This is about a remake of the 1995 Christmas ad: done in 2024, and the technique repeated in 2025.

The cost of both kinds of error is named as a timeline, and it is the one figure confirmed by the company's own leadership. An error used to cost a shooting day inside a year-long cycle; now it costs one generation inside a month-long one.

The first classical mode is shooting an ad of this technical complexity: a crew of fifty-plus, a year-long production cycle. A direct statement from the chief marketing officer: the production cycle collapsed from about a year to about a month. The second classical mode is reshooting the 1995 original the way it used to be done: one ad, one shoot, one production period, one budget.

The model against these modes. Over 70,000 generated video clips — a number named by the company itself, in its own behind-the-scenes production video. Five specialists at a contractor studio selected from and finished this batch in 30 days. And the number the case made it into the chapter for: about 100 people were employed across the campaign overall, roughly as many as a classical campaign of past years. The five were the ones pressing the button. The other ninety-five didn't go anywhere. Any framing along the lines of "five replaced fifty" is a distortion, and the course penalizes it separately.

A mandatory caveat about the number 70,000: this is the number of clips generated. How many of them made the final cut is not disclosed by any source. The common phrasing "70,000 clips for thirty seconds" is a conclusion dressed up as a fact, and it must not be used. What can be honestly calculated: a sixty-second ad, at an average shot length of about five seconds, runs roughly 12–20 shots, which puts the selection ratio on the order of one out of 3,500–5,800. This is a calculation, and it is named as one. The one place in public sources where the ratio of attempts to usable output is genuinely disclosed is a different case, and it cannot be carried over here; it is covered in this section's cost layer.

A second calculation, useful to the contractor: 70,000 clips of five seconds each, at a rate of $0.05–0.12 per second, is $17,500–42,000 in compute alone, before the five specialists' salaries, before licensing, before music, before finishing. For an ad whose classical budget runs into the millions, that's not much; for a contractor who took on "do it cheap with neural networks," it's the whole budget. For scale: the company's annual ad budget is about $5 billion.

The two years need to be kept apart: in retellings they blur together. The 2024 ad was the first attempt, made by three studios on four models; it is the one that drew the wave of public mockery, and it is the one the media measurement was done on. The 2025 ad was a repeat of the technique the following year, with a different split of work between contractors; it is the one an independent consumer panel gave the top score to. The numbers — 70,000 clips, five specialists, thirty days — belong to this campaign's production cycle; mixing the two years' audience scores together means comparing different things.

How this works. The input is text descriptions, the output is video clips of about five seconds each, then editing. Three studios and four different models; which four exactly, no source names — the primary source where that might have been stated sits behind a paywall and was not verified by direct reading. The gap is named. The fact "three studios and four models" itself is worth a separate look: it describes how the work was organized better than any price list. A classical campaign of this scale runs through one contractor with one pipeline, whereas here the client split the work across three independent studios and four different models. That means no single model closed out the task on its own, and part of the production decision was which piece to hand to whom. This also explains where the other ninety-five of the hundred people went: coordinating three contractors, reconciling material produced by four different methods, legal review, music, market adaptation. What the models were trained on is not disclosed.

The feedback loop is closed and measured from both sides — and the two sides disagreed. Commercial media measurement on the 2024 ad showed positive mentions falling from 12.2 to 3.4 percent and negative mentions rising from 13.5 to 53.4 percent, with the methodology of this measurement undisclosed — an honest gap. An independent consumer panel gave the 2025 repeat ad 5.9 out of a possible 5.9 points. A controlled measurement and an open audience diverged completely, and that has to be shown as it is.

The company declined to disclose the cost, saying only that it came out cheaper and faster than usual. The counter-claim that it cost about the same belongs to a third-party breakdown: neither side has an independent figure.

It's useful to compare this case with the previous one by the shape of the gain. At Kalshi, the gain is taken in money: forty thousand dollars of classical budget against two thousand. Here, the gain is taken in time: a year against a month, with the same number of people and an undisclosed budget. These are two different honest answers to the same question of "what got cheaper," and both are honest — on condition that the unit is named. Swapping the unit is the single most common error in retellings. You cannot say "cheaper" — there's no figure for it. You cannot say "fewer people" — it's the same number of them. What you can say is one thing: twelve times faster, by cycle.
