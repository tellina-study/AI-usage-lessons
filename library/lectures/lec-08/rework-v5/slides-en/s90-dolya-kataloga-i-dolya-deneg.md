## Visible slide text

**They measure supply and infer demand — those are different things.** Fraud fills the gap: on the music platform, 85% of listens to machine tracks are demonetized.

## Speaker notes

Everything said up to this frame has been inference drawn from worked cases. Here is something
else: a measurement. And it is stronger than inference, because it was obtained by four platforms
independently of each other, in different countries, by different methods, across different
modalities. A coincidence of result shape across such a set is rare, and when it happens, it is
worth using.

Deezer music platform, June 2026. The share of fully generated tracks in daily uploads passed
half — about ninety thousand tracks a day at the peak. The base, without which that first number
lies: in January 2025 the same share was a tenth, meaning the path from a tenth to a half took
eighteen months. And the second figure, without which the first lies even harder: consumption of
such music runs from one to three percent of all listens, and eighty-five percent of those listens
the platform itself flagged as fraud and demonetized. Half of what gets uploaded against one to
three percent of what gets listened to, six-sevenths of which is faked.

Steam game store, July 2026. The only one of the four industries where disclosing the use of
generative tools has been mandatory since January 2024 — meaning the number here is taken from
the games' own store pages. The share of the year's releases with disclosure ran from ten and
nine-tenths percent in 2024, through nineteen and nine-tenths in 2025, to thirty and eight-tenths
by July 2026. An independent count across fifty-three and a half thousand releases over three
years — mid-2023 to mid-2026 — gives the same series and adds the missing other half: games with
disclosure capture from ten to twenty-seven percent of estimated sales. The denominator has to be
named in full here, or the share hangs in the air: this is estimated sales of games released in
the same period, not the store's total revenue and not sales of the entire catalog; the figure
is an estimate, not audited platform revenue. There is a base over time too: in 2024 the same share
ran from three to six percent. A third of releases against a tenth to a quarter of the money.

This is a place that calls for attention to the denominator — exactly the exercise the course asks
about separately. Two different series circulate about Steam: the share of the whole catalog —
roughly one percent, through seven, to about twenty — and the share of the year's releases, the
series used above. These are different bases: the first denominator is every game in the store's
entire history, the second is only what came out in the given year. Putting them side by side as
the dynamics of one indicator means assembling a false pair, and it gets assembled in retellings
constantly. The frame carries one series, and the denominator is named in the same line.

US audiobooks, 2025 year-end. An industry sales survey and a consumer survey, both completed in
spring 2026. Publisher revenue — two and forty-three hundredths billion dollars, the fifteenth
straight year of growth. Active titles number over seven hundred fifty thousand, forty-three
percent more than a year earlier. And revenue from machine narration — three-hundredths of a
percent of sales. Only sixteen percent of listeners have ever listened to a book with a machine
voice, and willingness to try fell over the year from seventy percent to sixty-one. The catalog
grew nearly one and a half times over; the money did not move by three-hundredths of a percent.

Yandex Music, 2026 — the only independent Russian measurement in the lecture's material. A sample
of five and seven-tenths million profiles, a detector with ninety-seven and fourteen-hundredths
percent recall and eighty-three and seventy-seven-hundredths precision. The author of the
measurement names the method's own limit: the detector reads descriptions and accompanying
metadata, it does not listen to the sound, and so it does not count everything. Result: at least
one hundred forty thousand machine performers, about forty percent of new tracks a month, every
tenth chart track.

The same one line also carries the year's loudest headline — and, with it, an exercise in
denominators. A machine-generated country project took a song to number one on a digital sales
chart — the first time a machine-made song has topped a chart of this publication. The
denominator: this is the digital sales chart, the smallest by volume among the country charts,
and in the same week the main country chart and streaming were both topped by live performers.
The same piece of news, with a base and without one, gives two different conclusions.

What follows from this for an engineer. Volume of production has stopped being evidence of
demand: in all four cases, supply is what gets measured, and demand is what gets inferred. For any
channel where the unit cost falls nearly to zero, the behavior is now known in advance — supply
runs to the ceiling in months, demand stays put, and fraud immediately fills the gap. On the
music platform that third quantity is measured and equals eighty-five percent.
