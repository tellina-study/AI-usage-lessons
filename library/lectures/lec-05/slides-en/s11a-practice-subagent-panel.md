# Visible content

## Title bar
Rehearsing interview questions on AI personas

## Body
[what it is → how it works → with AI and without → where it breaks]

**WHAT IT IS**

Before going out to living people, the list of questions is run past several invented interviewees played by a model. The point is to catch bad questions before they spoil a real conversation. The run says nothing about the product. A rehearsal, not research.

**HOW IT WORKS**

1. Several different customer types are described — by how a person lives with the task: what they use now, what it costs them, what they once tried and dropped. **A character sketch is no use here.**
2. Each type is kept apart, so that the answers do not run together into one common voice.
3. The same list of questions is run past all of them.
4. Two things are read, **and the answers are not among them:** a question everyone answered the same way tells nobody apart; a question answered in terms of an imagined future invites a polite invention. Both get rewritten.

[Labelled diagram on the right: five interviewee boxes in a row, the links between them struck through — "the invented interviewees do not hear each other"; an arrow down to a gold box "the only thing that leaves is a corrected list of questions"]

**WITH AI AND WITHOUT**

| WITHOUT AI — how the rehearsal has always been run | WITH AI — what changed |
|---|---|
| The list of questions is talked through with a colleague, or one live interview out of eight is spent on the rehearsal. It works, but it gives one point of view and costs a day. | The rehearsal takes minutes, and a dozen runs are affordable. **Only the rehearsal** gets cheaper: nothing replaces the interview with a living person. |

[Gold callout — WHERE IT BREAKS]
The interviewees' answers are not data about users: there is one model, and five interviewees give five views of that one model. There are no five people behind them. Checked against a real national survey, the model reproduces the average answers and halves the spread of opinion (**16 against 31**). If a conclusion about the product has been carried out of the run, the practice has been run wrong: its output is a corrected question.

## Speaker notes

The idea of running a conversation past several interviewees played by a model comes up often. Mechanically it is sound, on one condition: the role of the practice is narrow, and that narrowness has to be held.

What it is, in plain words. You have written a list of questions for an interview and you are about to take it to living people. Before you go, you run that list past several invented interviewees played by a model. This is a rehearsal — much like a read-through of a play before opening night: people listen for where the text does not work.

Then, step by step. First you describe several different customer types, and you describe them without a character sketch. A sketch of the kind "Anna, thirty-four, likes convenient interfaces" is useless, because nothing follows from it. What is useful is different: what the person uses now, what that costs them in hours or in money, what they once tried and dropped. Each type is kept apart so the answers do not run together into one averaged voice. The same list is run past all of them.

And from there the whole value of the practice is in where you look. Not at the content of the answers. At two things. The first: which questions produced roughly the same answer from everyone. A question like that tells nobody apart, and in a live interview it will waste time — it gets rewritten. The second: which questions are answered in terms of an imagined future, "I would use this". That is exactly the breach of the rule about the concrete past, and it is wired into the wording of the question itself; catching it in rehearsal is cheaper than letting it spoil a live conversation.

The practice invented nothing new: questions were talked through with a colleague, or on the first live interview, long before any models. AI made the rehearsal cheap — it now takes minutes. A conversation with a person costs what it always did.

Now the boundary, without which the practice turns into the failure we take apart next. The only thing that leaves is a corrected list of questions. The interviewees' answers do not go into a decision and are not quoted as data. That is how the practice is built: there is one model. Five such interviewees are five views of one model. There are no five people behind them. A check against a real national survey puts a number on it: the model reproduces the averages and understates the spread of opinion by almost half. A collapsed spread is false confidence exactly where living people differ most.
