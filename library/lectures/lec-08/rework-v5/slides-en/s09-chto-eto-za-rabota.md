## Visible slide text

**The stage has one decision — approve the look before the big spending starts.** Before 2022, the classics ran through four tiers: in every case what got cheaper was executing the decision, not the choice itself.

**The section pays for selection, rights, discernment.**

## Speaker notes

Before any example, you need to understand exactly what the model is intervening in. The stage is
responsible for three adjacent products, which production keeps strictly distinct because they
have different clients, deadlines, and prices. Concept art — agree on the look; it never goes
outward, it lives inside production and dies once the decision is made. Illustration — goes into
the product: an article, a cover, an ad banner, a product card. Key frame — a single image that
sets the visual language for an entire campaign, from a billboard to a banner in an app. You
cannot conflate them when counting savings: a hundred concepts cost the same as one finished
illustration, because a concept is never finished.

And before moving on to the numbers, it's worth naming the section's own plan: once execution got
cheap down to zero, the business pays for three things — selection, rights, discernment. This
section unfolds the first two: first a breakdown of selection on the unit price and on the
editorial team's home GPU, then rights — what the model was trained on and what the court
decided. Discernment — a signature that confirms itself — is only named here; its full breakdown
comes in the section on voice, where voice forgery is the plot itself.

What goes in: a verbal description of the task — what's depicted, in what mood, for what brand,
under what constraints; attached to it are references, brand guidelines, and technical
requirements. The question being answered here is shorter than it looks: "what does it look
like," as opposed to "what is it" (a drawing) and "how much of it" (a table). This boundary will
come back as its own slide, in the place where we discuss why a generator doesn't replace
computer-aided design.

There are four ways to get this kind of material, and before 2022 there were exactly four:
commissioning a freelancer, something ready-made from a library, your own catalog shoot (not
replaceable by generation by the very definition of the task — you need this exact physical
object), or assembly from parts in an editor. Generation became the fifth way starting in 2022.

The stage makes one decision: approve the look before the big money is spent. That's exactly why
the stage sits at the start of the production order — an error here is cheap to fix and expensive
to let through further down the cycle. And from this comes the observation the whole section
grows out of: the stage's economics rest on a single ratio — how many variants you can afford to
look at before picking one. Before 2022, that ratio was set by the price of a single variant and
was small — three to four sketches, rarely more.

The classics ran through four tiers, each with a published price, and the unit is the same for
all of them — the price of one usable picture. Commissioning a freelancer: a standard assignment
from a mid-level freelancer — $50–150 with a 1–3 day turnaround; the hourly rate from an analysis
of a thousand marketplace profiles — $49.65. Inside the same tier — the Russian variant: from a
sample of 3,848 Moscow illustrator profiles, a digital illustration starts at 3,860 ₽, an ad
banner at 500 ₽; a caveat is mandatory — this is "starting at," the lower bound of the published
rates, not the market average, and if you take it as the average, the Russian classics come out
artificially cheap. The second tier — a commissioned catalog shoot — hundreds of dollars, used
where you need this exact object, and not replaceable by generation by the very definition of the
task. The third tier — microstock of the 2000s: $0.25–1 apiece, against "from several hundred to
several thousand" at the classical agencies before it; a three-order-of-magnitude collapse in the
per-unit price had already happened once in this industry, twenty years earlier, and the market
survived, splitting into "cheap and plentiful" and "expensive and exactly this." The fourth
tier — the in-house designer: there's no price, there's a salary and a workload; this looks like a
trivial detail, but this is exactly the mode that turns out to be the classical one for a
corporate client, and it's exactly the one with almost no public numbers. One of the section's
cases runs right into this wall: the case is measured down to the last minute of training on the
model's side, and uncounted on the classical side, because companies don't publish their in-house
designer's internal rate.

Two points from a sixty-year chain stand here as a reminder that this isn't the first such
conversation, and both are built the same way. 1961, "101 Dalmatians": before xerography, the
inking department manually traced the animator's pencil drawing onto celluloid by hand — an
intermediary operation in its purest form, and what was paid for was precisely the accuracy of
the transfer. Xerography transferred the drawing to celluloid directly: the film's headcount came
out to roughly half of "Sleeping Beauty" (1959). A detail that says more about the nature of such
transitions than the numbers do: the story of ninety-nine spotted puppies was chosen precisely
because the new method made it feasible — hand-tracing every spot would have been unreal. The
tool determined the content. And right alongside it — what moved to the right: the recognizable,
sloppy linework of the sixties, which ink used to clean up; Walt Disney publicly disliked it.

1990, Photoshop 1.0, released February 19. Before it, professional retouching ran on dedicated
Scitex-class systems at around $300 an hour; a Photoshop license cost $895 one time — it paid for
itself in under three hours of Scitex work. The drop in hourly price — roughly three orders of
magnitude — and the prepress shop disappeared along with it. The retoucher, meanwhile, didn't go
anywhere: they moved to a different tool. What moved to the right here is the same thing, only
noticeably smaller: a retouch stopped being an event that someone commissioned, paid for, and
signed off on — and from this grows the entire subsequent argument about whether a published
photo can be trusted. This argument isn't closed even now, and in the sections on voice and on
provenance it will come back in exactly the same form.

What the two points have in common is exactly the form they're here for. The operation
disappeared, not the profession. The volume produced grew faster than the price fell. And in both
cases a dispute arose about the authenticity of the result, which wasn't closed by a technical
argument — it closed because a generation changed and the new norm stopped looking disputable.

It's against this background that what follows has to be read. The first impulse will be to
place generation in the same series as a third point — and that impulse has to be checked, not
accepted. The check comes back negative: generation belongs to a different order, because what's
getting cheaper isn't executing a decision already made, but the moment before the decision — the
cost of a failed attempt disappeared, not the cost of transferring from one medium to another.
But to see that distinction, you first have to take a proper look at what real drops in the cost
of execution actually look like — and that's what this slide is for.
