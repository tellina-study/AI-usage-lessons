## Speaker notes

Four astronauts sit in the Dragon capsule two hundred meters from the International Space Station. The commander keeps his hands on the touchscreen — not to fly the ship, but to be ready to take control if something goes wrong. The ship docks itself to the docking port without a single command from the screen: laser rangefinders measure the distance dozens of times per second, automation calculates and issues commands to the attitude-control thrusters, and a few minutes later the latches click softly shut. This is not a test and not a demonstration — a routine operation that has repeated since 2020. The crew watches something happen that, until recently, required a pilot's painstaking manual work.

This scene opens the section neither by accident nor for decoration: the lecture's first substantive section was built on one and the same form — first the term and a general description of the stage, then the breakdowns. After that opening, the form starts to become predictable, and here we deliberately break it. The term and the general description of teleoperation will follow further on, only after the scene has shown what this looks like in practice.

This break in form is not decoration but a substantive device: the scene shows exactly the tension around which the whole section is built — automation acts, the human watches and stays ready to intervene — before that tension gets broken down into terms and definitions. By the time we reach the formal description of teleoperation on the next slide, you have already seen it in action and can check the definition against the example you have already seen.
