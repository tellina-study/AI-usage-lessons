# Requests Duplicate

## Assertion

Olga answers a client's question on Monday morning. Two hours later the same question arrives again — in different words. She doesn't recognize the rephrasing and processes it as new.

## Visual

1. Monday morning. Olga, the training center's administrator, opens the requests table and sees a question from a client: "when can I pay for the class?" She answers.
2. Two hours later — a second request from the same contact: "how do I pay for the course". In meaning, it's the same question. Olga doesn't recognize the rephrasing and processes it as new: answers again.
3. The client gets two different answers from the center two hours apart — and it looks as if the first answer got lost.

> **This isn't rare.** By Olga's own estimate, such repeats happen about 10 times a day: someone forgot they'd already asked, someone didn't see the answer and asked again in different words.

> **The cost of the mistake isn't abstract.** Double the administrator's work — plus the client's distrust: "don't they see I already asked?"

## Speaker notes

The need is simple, and it's exactly the one this case starts with: Olga, the training center's administrator, keeps track of requests one by one, all day long. On Monday morning she sees a question from a client sitting in the table — "when can I pay for the class?" — processes it and answers. An ordinary morning, an ordinary request, nothing about it that stands out.

Two hours later a second request arrives from the same contact: "how do I pay for the course". In meaning it's the same question, just phrased differently. But Olga sees dozens of requests a day and doesn't keep in her head who asked what two hours ago — she doesn't recognize the rephrasing and processes the request as new: spends the time again, answers again, and nothing in front of her flags that this already happened once.

For the client it looks different. He gets two different answers from the training center two hours apart — and the only explanation that comes to mind is that the first answer got lost or nobody read it. The cost of this mistake isn't abstract: it's double the administrator's work, which could have gone to something else, and the client's distrust — now he's not sure anyone is even reading carefully, and that doubt doesn't go away just because the second answer eventually arrives.

And this isn't a one-off. By Olga's own sense of it, this kind of repeat — the same question in different words, already answered — happens to her about ten times a day. We haven't measured that number with instruments; it comes from a person who reads requests every day, not from a dashboard — but even as an estimate it shows this isn't a quirk of one morning, it's a standing part of the flow, day after day.

Today the task isn't whether the check is needed — it obviously is, since this happens every day — but what to build it from so it catches not only literal repetition but also the rephrasing we've just run into.
