# No faster — the same forty files

## Assertion

The subagent opens all forty files and reads them for the same length of time. What it wins by is two other things: the reading stays in its single-use context, and the list of four places comes back into the session the edit sits in.

## Visual

| Option | What it does | What it does not do / the limit |
|---|---|---|
| read it yourself, in this same session | full control over what gets read and in what order | the session's context is taken up by the contents of forty files, of which four are needed for the edit |
| a second session | the working context stays clean: the forty files land in somebody else's | the list will have to be carried over by hand, and the agent will edit from your retelling — the condition about the old accounts is the first thing to fall out of a retelling |
| call in a colleague | the only option that can answer in a minute — if the colleague is fresh on the subject | the only option that depends on somebody else's schedule: it is decided by whether the person is free right now |
| hand it to a subagent | the forty files are opened and closed in a separate, single-use context; the list comes back on its own, into the same session | it does not get faster: the subagent opens and reads all forty files, the reading time is the same |
| edit from memory | instantly | the fourth place — the client-side check — is not remembered by everyone who wrote it |

> **Conclusion.** The twenty minutes of reading from the scene stay twenty minutes: forty files are forty files, wherever they are read. Two things change. First, whose context holds them: the subagent's context closes along with the subagent. Second, where the list comes back to: from a subagent it arrives on its own, into the session the edit sits in; out of a second session you carry it over yourself.

## Speaker notes

It is worth starting from what was said. "Faster" — that gets tested by counting, row by row.

**Read it yourself, in this same session.** What it gives: full control over what gets read and in what order. What it costs: the session's context is taken up by the contents of forty files, of which four are needed for the edit; the other thirty-six sit in it as dead weight until the session ends.

**A second session.** What it gives: the working context stays clean, the forty files land in somebody else's. What it costs: the list will have to be carried over by hand, and the agent will edit from your retelling. The condition about the old accounts is the first thing to fall out of a retelling.

**A colleague.** What it gives: the only option that can answer in a minute, if the person is fresh on the subject. What it costs: the only option that depends on somebody else's schedule — it is decided by whether they are free right now.

**A subagent.** What it gives: the forty files are opened and closed in a separate, single-use context, and the list comes back on its own, into the same session. What it costs: it does not get faster.

**Edit from memory.** What it gives: instantly. What it costs: the fourth place, the client-side check, is not remembered by everyone who wrote it.

Now about "faster" — here is the turn of the whole case. The subagent opens all forty files and reads them through. The twenty minutes of reading from the scene stay twenty minutes: not a single line is read faster because another process is reading it. Forty files are forty files, wherever they are read.

Let us take this all the way, because this is where the guess breaks. While the subagent is reading forty files, you are waiting — the same twenty minutes you would have waited reading them in your own session. Delegation does not shorten the wait and never promised to shorten it: it changes the place the reading ends up in. That is exactly why the test of the guess is a count of files and not a measurement of speed: the reading speed is identical in both options by construction, and there is nothing to argue about there.

The gain lies in two other places, and they are worth naming precisely. First: the reading stays in the subagent's context, and that context closes along with the subagent — in the working session nothing is left of the forty files but four addresses. Second: the list comes back to where the edit sits, on its own, without your retelling. A second session gives you the first of the two and leaves the second to you.

From there comes the answer to "why set up a subagent at all, then, if the time is the same". For the sake of what stays available further on in this same session. The twenty minutes of reading went into the work — the list was needed. The half hour of explaining everything a second time did not have to be spent at all, and that is exactly what a subagent saves. The saving is counted in what is left in the context after the answer; the minutes of reading do not enter that count at all.

The objection "retelling is nothing, I'll just copy the whole answer across" is settled by arithmetic. Copy it whole and the working context receives exactly what you were getting away from, plus your time spent on the transfer. The shorter what you carry over, the more of it is lost; the longer it is, the less point there was in taking the reading away. No middle ground is provided for in that count.

The objection "and what if you launch several subagents at once, is it faster then?" — yes, and that is the second case of this rung in its entirety. What gets faster there is a queue of several independent tasks; the reading of one question stays the same. For a single search across forty files there is no gain in time at any number of subagents.

And about the colleague, put in a row of their own even though they are not a tool. In real life they are the first candidate and often the right one, and the row is there so that this gets said directly: otherwise the option looks forgotten, and the choice between four machine methods starts to look complete.
