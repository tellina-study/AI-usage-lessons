## Speaker notes

Both halves of the pair have now been presented, and it's worth placing them fully side by side. The section on 3D showed "The Wizard of Oz" screened on a spherical display: over ninety percent of the frame touched by the model, a 4:3 frame filled in on the sides, things invented that Victor Fleming never shot, and more than 2.2 million tickets sold for the 1939 film. The previous frame showed the Herculaneum scroll: zero percent invented, not a single generated letter, and text that otherwise no one would ever have read.

The technique is a close neighbor in both cases. In both, a trained model restores an image that is not in the source data: on the tomogram there are no visible letters until the recognizer shows where the ink is; in the 4:3 frame there are no edges of the set until the model fills them in. The architecture is close, the training is the same, and the engineering task is formulated in similar words.

The ethics are opposite, and the difference fits in one sentence: in the first case the model answers a question that has a checkable answer; in the second, a question that has no answer and is therefore decided by taste.

The boundary between restoration and invention runs exactly here, and it is drawn by one criterion: does a way exist to check the result independently of whoever obtained it. Neither the technology nor the share touched plays any part in this. The word read is checked by Greek philology, by neighboring columns, and by comparison with other scrolls from the same collection — and if the recognizer made a mistake, a philologist with no connection to the team that trained it will catch it. The filled-in edge of the set is checked by nothing: the original of that edge does not exist, and the only criterion for its correctness is plausibility — that is, the taste of whoever made it.

This distinction is worth testing on a third case, so it doesn't look tailored to fit two examples. Upscaling an archival photograph: the model draws in details that are not in the source file, and there is nothing to check them against — a negative with more detail does not exist. So the task is artistic, however carefully it's done. And restoring a missing measurement in a data series, where neighboring measurements are known and the physics of the process is given: the predicted value is checked both by the model of the process and by a subsequent direct measurement. So the task is restorative. The share altered can be identical in both cases; what separates them is independent checkability, and nothing else.

From this comes a practical rule for choice, wider than both cases and applicable far beyond cultural heritage. Before applying a generative model to the restoration of anything — an archival record, a damaged document, a lost data fragment, a missing measurement — ask what the result will be checked against. If there is a ground truth or an independent way to cross-check, the task is restorative, and a model is appropriate for it. If there is no ground truth, the task is artistic, and the result should be called that, honestly and in the same words in which it will later be sold.

The percentage touched is beside the point here. The ninety-percent case would have been just as valid had it been sold as a new work based on the original: a viewer who bought a ticket for a "new reading" would have gotten exactly what they paid for. It is sold as a screening of the film — and it is exactly in this gap between what was done and what it was called that the whole objection lies. Not in the technology, not in the share.

The same pair, where the technique is one and the purpose is opposite, has already appeared twice in the evening: splitting a recording into separate sources, against generating a voice that never sounded. This is not a coincidence. It shows where the dividing line actually runs along which an engineer makes a decision about applicability: it runs between a question with a checkable answer and a question without one, and the quality of the model has nothing to do with it.
