# The condition nobody wrote down

## Assertion

Of four moves one works — a shared description of the task that both of them read before starting. A coordinator adds cost faster than it lowers risk.

## Visual

| Move | What it does | What it does not do / the limit |
|---|---|---|
| give both pieces to one subagent | one worker sees both ends and keeps the composition of the fields consistent by itself | gives up the parallelism where it was real and free; on a large task one worker becomes the bottleneck |
| give each of them broad access — let them work out the neighboring piece themselves | sometimes it works: a worker finds the neighboring setting and adapts | broad access means a broad context and broad permissions; reading the neighboring piece does not yet mean understanding it the same way |
| a shared description of the task that both read before starting | the composition of what is shared is named once for both, and a divergence is visible before the work | the description has to be written and maintained: there appear cycles of reading, editing and agreeing, and that is a real cost |
| set up a third subagent as a coordinator | checks the two finished jobs against each other | adds a layer of coordination on top of two that have already diverged; the reconciling will have to be done anyway, just by somebody else's hands |

> **Conclusion.** In the layout the condition was named out loud: which pieces are independent and why got said. Here nobody wrote the shared description down, and neither of the two could have worked it out — nobody knew the whole task. The third move works, and it works by the same thing the layout worked by.

## Speaker notes

Four moves, and one works. Let us go through all four, because the three that do not work look convincing.

**Give both pieces to one subagent.** An honest move: one worker sees both ends at once and keeps the composition of the fields consistent simply because it sets that composition up itself. The cost is named directly — the parallelism is given up, and given up where it was real and cost nothing. On two pieces that is bearable; on a task of fifteen pieces one worker becomes the bottleneck, and the whole layout loses its point.

**Give each of them broad access — let them work out the neighboring piece themselves.** Sometimes it works: the worker opens the neighboring setting, sees the composition of the fields and adapts. The cost is hidden in the word "broad": broad access means a broad context and broad permissions — exactly what a trimmed tool list restricts. And the move has a second limit, less obvious: reading the neighboring piece does not yet mean understanding it the same way. Two workers reading one and the same thing diverge in interpretation about as easily as they diverged before reading it.

**A shared description of the task that both read before starting.** This one works. The composition of what is shared — the names of the fields, their set, the required flags, what counts as done — is named once for both and sits apart from both pieces. A divergence becomes visible before the work: it is enough to check a configuration against the description rather than two configurations against each other. The move has one condition — the description is written before the pieces have diverged, and maintained for as long as they live. The cost is worth naming honestly, or the move looks free: there appear cycles of reading, editing and agreeing on the description itself, they take noticeable time, and they are paid every time the task changes.

**Set up a third subagent as a coordinator.** It looks like a solution: it checks the two finished jobs against each other. The cost grows faster than the risk falls. On top of two jobs that have already diverged there is added a third worker with its own context and its own understanding of the task, and the divergence will have to be reconciled anyway — just by somebody else's hands and one step later. The move is tested by one question: what does the coordinator do that could not be written into a shared description before the work started. No answer — it is a patch.

Now about what the third move works by — and here it converges with the layout. On Friday the condition was named out loud: which pieces are independent and why got said. Here nobody wrote it down, and there was nobody to work it out: breaking the work up narrowed each one's context, and neither of the two knew the whole task. The third move names the condition out loud before it is needed — by the same movement as the layout.

"Write a shared description" looks more modest than it sounds: this is not project documentation but a few lines about what the pieces have in common — the names, the composition, the sign of being done. Writing it out in full in advance is pointless: a description that has grown into a tome stops being read, and it goes out of date faster than the task does. A sensible length is the one a worker reads through in full before working and does not skip.

How exactly a description like that is written so that it covers both sides — on the next screen.
