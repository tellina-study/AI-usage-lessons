## Visible slide text

**Step 2's column — applied identically throughout the lecture:**
**(a)** both modes are given a number in the same unit · **(b)** the second mode is named but has no number of its own · **(c)** a second mode simply doesn't exist for this task, and the reason is stated.

**A gain measured from zero (ignoring what the classical mode could already do) is not accepted.**

## Speaker notes

Every detailed case study later in this lecture is built on the same form. Let's work through it once on an empty example, so we don't have to explain it again later.

Step 1. The cost of both kinds of error, named before any precision. What does a decision made too slowly cost, and what does a decision made too riskily cost — in hours, in sols, in dollars per kilogram, in human risk. Until this is named, the gain doesn't read as anything.

Step 2. Both classical modes, calculated in the same units. This is where step 2's column applies, used identically throughout the lecture: (a) both modes are given a number in the same unit as the model; (b) the second mode is named but has no number of its own, and that's said on the spot, not disguised; (c) a second mode simply doesn't exist for this task, and the reason for that is stated explicitly, not presented as a gap in the research.

Step 3. The model or the automation against these modes. A comparison in the same units, with a named base. A gain measured from zero isn't accepted in this lecture; what matters is the difference specifically from what worked before.

Step 4. How it's built — input, output, trained or classical, the product's name. Where there's no answer in open sources, that's stated honestly as "sources don't disclose this" — rather than filled in with a similar-looking number from a neighboring case.

Step 5. What moved over. By the name of a form from the course's own dictionary, or an explicit refusal to assign a form, with a reason. Next to it — a "gave — cost" pair: what became possible, with a number and a base, and what it was paid for with, in comparable units. We'll apply this form thirteen times in a row from here — to AEGIS, SPOC, COROB-X, LeoLabs, and so on — and keeping it in mind will save us from having to explain each next case study's structure from scratch.
