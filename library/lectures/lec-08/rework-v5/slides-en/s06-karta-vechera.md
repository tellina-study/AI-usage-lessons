## Visible slide text

**The rows are set up front. A column gets added at the end of each section.**

## Speaker notes

This is the one table that lives through the whole evening. It is presented six times, and each
time it has one more column. Right now only the rows are labeled, and that is enough: without
rows, cells mean nothing, while the rows are set once and never change after that.

There are six rows, and they are chosen by the type of decision that has to be made. The
difference from sorting by tool is substantial. Cut by tool, and you get a list of products that
will be out of date by next semester. Cut by task type, and you get a grid that a new product
simply drops into as a cell — and that is the kind of knowledge that survives a change of model
generations.

Generate a first approximation is the stage where you need many cheap variants and not one of
them is meant to go straight into the product. A concept, a rough look, a trial voice, a draft
animation. This is exactly where the model moved in first and hardest, and the reason is exactly
the economics of the attempt: the whole stage used to be built around the question "how many
variants can we afford to look at before choosing one," and the model changed the very quantity
the stage was built around.

Bring to production quality is the stage everything moved to. Selection, culling, finishing,
release. This is new work: when a trial cost money, it was done sparingly, and there was almost
nothing to look at. Now there are thousands of trials, and this cell holds the answer to where
the money went.

Restore what already exists stands as its own row, because it is a different task, and it keeps
getting confused with generation. Upscaling archival film, pulling a voice out of a 1960s mono
recording, reading a charred scroll — here the model does not invent, it reconstructs something
already recorded. The argument around restoration runs far calmer than the argument around
generation, and that is not a coincidence: restoration has an original you can check the result
against.

Hold exact measurements is the row that an engineering audience will find the most important, and
it will fill in mostly with refusals. Size, tolerance, a checkable condition — these are
questions an image of an object does not answer, because the image itself carries no units of
measurement and no construction tree. A cell that reads "a model is not the right tool here" is
a full-fledged result, not a gap, and the map will have several such cells.

Fit the real-time budget is the row about the frame's deadline, not the task's deadline. Between
"a picture in twenty seconds" and "a frame in sixteen milliseconds" lies not a difference in
speed but a difference in the class of engineering problem, and this will become visible where
the talk turns to the 3D scene.

Answer "who is accountable" is the row about provenance and rights. Where did this detail come
from, can it be released, who owns the result, what confirms that it was shot rather than
assembled. This row will come up empty for the still image — and empty by how the method is
built, not because nothing was found.

There will be five columns, added one at a time at the end of each section, in order of rising
unit price: image, sound, video, 3D, recognition. There is deliberately no sixth column: the
Wrap-up is not a modality, it reads the finished map rather than adding to it. Recognition stands
apart in the row for a different reason too: there, the model solves the inverse problem — it
reads rather than draws — and it is the same attention architecture, turned to face the other way.

A separate band will run below the grid — the payment address. Each breakdown names where the
payment went: to selection, to rights, or to discernment — and by the end of the evening it will
be visible that the addresses are distributed unevenly across modalities, and that unevenness is
itself meaningful.

One last thing about this map, and it is about honesty. By the end of the evening, part of the
grid will stay empty. Every empty cell will be named for a reason: either "the method does not
fit here, and here is why," or "the technique works, but no case with both sides counted could be
found in open sources." There will be no "surely something got missed" hedge on this map — a gap
is named by its own name, or not named at all.
