## Speaker notes

This section has just named two of its forms — the workload moved to a different step, and the performer changed. Here is the list they were drawn from. Every breakdown from here on ends with a named form — or a named refusal to assign one, and a refusal is as much a verdict as a name. There are no numbers in this glossary on purpose: a count under each form will build up from the breakdowns, and by the end of the conversation seven of the nine forms will carry a measured value, while two will not.

This glossary of forms came from the previous lecture, and proved its worth there too. The familiar image — "the workload moved to the right" — lies in several directions at once. It describes one case out of nine possible ones and silently substitutes it for the other eight. Work can move to a different step while keeping its full volume. It can double while staying exactly where it was. It can disappear, and that is the rarest of the cases. It can appear for the first time — nobody did this work before at all. It can fall to a different hand at the same place and the same volume. It can stay exactly the same, with only the consequence of a miss changing. And it can stay exactly the same in everything except the condition of the hand doing it. Nine rows exist for exactly one reason: so these cases do not collapse into one.

The glossary splits by the question asked of it. The first five forms measure place and volume: where the work now sits and how much of it there is. Four and four-a are a pair with opposite signs at one place: the workload either grew or fell, and both halves genuinely occur, sometimes on the same measurement. The next four measure the hand, the order, the consequence of a miss, and the condition of the hand itself. This split is not a formality: the last form carries the most uncomfortable observation of the lecture — the performer stayed the same, the place stayed the same, the volume stayed the same, and the result got worse.

The eighth form was introduced by this topic, and before it there was no glossary with this axis. It is easy to confuse with its two neighbors, so the distinction is worth memorizing right away. In the fifth form the hand changed: a different person does the work. In four-a the volume fell: there is less work. In the eighth, the hand and the volume are the same, and what fell is the quality of what that hand finds. A quick check is the question of what the share is computed from. Four-a has no share at all — it counts the absolute volume of work. The eighth has a share, and it is computed from the same thing: the same cases, the same people, a different share of findings.

Row eight-a is kept empty on purpose, and it needs to be read as empty. The reverse case — the same performer getting better without the assistant present — sounds just as plausible as the eighth, and there is not a single measurement behind it. A number is reserved for it, a name is given, there is no count. This empty row cannot be papered over with a similar case: any case where a person got better while the assistant was switched on measures something else — it measures the pair, not the hand.

Now for what the glossary of forms is not. It does not answer the question of whether something is good or bad. A form only names what happened to the work; whether it is a benefit or a cost is decided separately, by a number and a baseline. A workload move to a different step can be a pure benefit, when there is less of it at the new step, or a pure cost, when there is nobody to pay for it at the new step. The same holds for a drop in volume at the same place: one and the same visual-search measurement shows both a benefit and a cost, depending on whether the cue was correct or not.

And a last point, which is why this glossary is issued right here, at the start. Alongside it works a second tagging scheme — four payment addresses: attention, skill, signature, arbitration. These two things answer different questions about the same case, and must not be confused. A form says what happened to the work: where it now sits, how much of it there is, whose hand does it, what a miss ends in. An address says at which human step this happened: does a person review it second, did they stop doing it themselves, did they sign something unread, do they work through a disagreement. Work is capable of moving in a way no human step feels at all — and a human is capable of paying at a point where the work stayed exactly in place.

And the distinction without which the glossary reads wrong: a form says what happened to the work, and a payment address says at which human step it happened. The nine names answer the first question, the four addresses answer the second, and they must not be mixed: work moves in a way no human step feels, and a human pays at a point where the work stayed in place.
