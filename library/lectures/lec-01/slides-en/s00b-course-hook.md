# The main question of the course is not "can we use AI?", but "should we, and where?"

## Assertion

The main question of the course is not "can we use AI?", but "should we, and where?"

## Visual

On the left, a large 3-level funnel, each level noticeably narrower than the previous one (a classic narrowing funnel): the top wide block "100% of AI pilots get launched", the middle one narrower with a minus "−90% roll back", the bottom narrowest gold block "10% reach production" (two-line text inside the narrow block). On the right — the main idea of the course in large type (24pt): "Tomorrow — almost everywhere. Today — almost no one. This course is about that gap" + the central question of the course (32pt, gold accent): "Where does AI work, where doesn't it, and how do you tell?". The source context (Gartner / CNews) in small type at the top of the right column.

## Speaker notes

The question "can we use AI" in 2026 is no longer interesting — we can. Nine hundred million people use ChatGPT every week, and half of all developers use it every day. This is no longer "should we get started", it is already the infrastructure that other products run on.

But the question "should we, and where" is open. According to aggregated data from business publications, about ninety percent of corporate AI pilots in Russia never reach full production deployment: thirty to forty percent are shut down with no effect, and only seven to ten percent make it to production. This is not a problem unique to Russia — McKinsey and Gartner regularly publish similar figures for other markets. Corporate AI projects everywhere run into the gap between demo readiness and production reliability.

This combination — mass adoption and mass failure — forms the central engineering task of the course. The main question is this: where does AI work, where does it not, and how do you tell in advance. This is a diagnostic question, not a triumphant one. The course does not promise a recipe for success; it gives you a way to distinguish applications where AI is appropriate from applications where it creates the illusion of a solution. Budget, time, system reliability, and sometimes people's safety depend on that distinction.

We will return to this central question in every lecture of the course.
