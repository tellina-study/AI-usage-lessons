## Visible slide text

**A decision about a person outlives the person who made it.** The physician will go on leave, change jobs, forget; the patient will turn up a year later at a different institution. The only thing that crosses between them is the text.

**A case book, 1819.** Name, age, diagnosis, how long the complaint has lasted, what was prescribed, an entry for the next day.

**Two hundred years later, these are the same fields** — only now their composition and the order they're kept in are set by a government order, not by a physician's habit.

## Speaker notes

Why keep medical documentation at all. A decision about a person outlives the person who made it: the physician will go on leave, change jobs, forget, while the patient will turn up a year later at a different institution — and the only thing that crosses between them is the text. The question sounds naive, and the answer usually given is wrong: "for reporting," "for the inspector," "because that's how it's done." If that were really the reason, this section wouldn't be worth five minutes.

On screen is a page from the case book of the Philadelphia Almshouse infirmary, eighteen nineteen. John Bartholomew, twenty-seven years old, diabetes. Then, in the physician's own hand: has suffered about eighteen months, doesn't know the cause, was strong and full-bodied before the illness, passes three to four quarts of water a day, was nearly cured in August by a country doctor with a strict meat diet and iron pills, the illness returned after exposure to cold and fatigue. Pulse somewhat excited, weakness in the loins, sleeps well, has lost a great deal of flesh. Prescribed. The next entry, the following day: it has acted, feels relief, urine deeply colored, the quantity hasn't decreased, pulse as yesterday.

Look at what's here. Name and age. Diagnosis. Duration. What came before, and how it was treated. What the exam found. What was prescribed. And, under a separate date, what came of it. These are exactly the fields that sit in today's standardized form, the same ones we just broke down step by step. Two hundred years, a different country, a different medicine, a pen instead of a keyboard — the design of the record is the same.

Why is it the same? Because the record has one job, and it hasn't changed. One person makes a decision about someone, and another will act on it — a week from now, a year from now, somewhere else. The physician will go on leave, change jobs, forget this visit among a thousand others. The patient will come back, and the person sitting across from them won't have seen them before. The only thing that crosses between these two people is the text. Not memory, not an impression, not a conversation. The text.

Everything else in this section follows from here. If the record is the only channel, then its completeness matters more than its brevity, and any tool that makes the document shorter has to be checked against exactly that. If decisions get acted on from the record, then an error in it costs not a reprimand but an action. And if the text is now written by someone other than the person who spoke with the patient — that's exactly the change this section exists to examine.

One clarification, so it doesn't sound like nothing has changed. Something has: in eighteen nineteen, the composition of the record rested on a physician's own habit and the school they trained in. Today, standardized forms and the order they're kept in are set by a Ministry of Health order, and this is no longer a matter of the writer's own taste. Later in this section we'll see what follows from this for the tool: the model has somewhere to put the text, but it isn't the model that assigns where it goes.

I'll also say what's missing from this page, which is no less interesting. There are no units of measurement beyond quarts of water a day. No scales, no scores, no threshold. This physician's decision is recorded in words, and the only way to check it is to read it. This feature of the stage stays with us for the rest of the section: a score has a threshold; a record doesn't. You can't say "this record is seventy percent complete" and make a decision on that number. This directly explains why scoring a machine-produced document is so hard: there's nothing to measure it by except a human reading it — and we'll see later that every measurement in this section runs up against exactly that.

And one note for anyone from IT who's used to a different order of things. The record here isn't an event log. A log is written for whoever will debug a failure, and it's written mechanically, completely, with no selection at all. A medical record is written by a person, for a person who will act on it, and so it holds not everything, but what's been selected — what the writer judged to matter. Selection is the substance of the work. A tool that takes typing the text off the human's hands doesn't take selection off them.
