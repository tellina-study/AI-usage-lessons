## Speaker notes

Before diving into the individual architectures, it helps to see the route as a whole. The lecture consists of six sections, and they are arranged not as a catalog of technologies but as a single line of reasoning. Section zero is the opening: the Air Canada case, which shows that the cost of the wrong architecture choice is quite real. The first section is the prompt and its limits: the cheapest and most predictable way to apply a model, a single call, and where it hits its ceiling. The second is RAG, retrieval-augmented generation, an add-on built on the semantic search from the previous lecture. The third is fine-tuning a model and its honest place in the lineup of "prompt vs RAG vs fine-tuning." The fourth and largest is agents: how a model becomes a component of a system through API and MCP, what an assistant agent is assembled from, where its memory lives, and how all of this breaks. And the fifth is assembly: the ladder of architectural complexity, the choice route, and a short checklist you can apply to a new task in a couple of minutes.

Running through all six sections is one keystone line, and it is worth keeping this map in front of your eyes for its sake. It is not "learn six technologies" but "learn to choose which of them to apply to a specific task — and recognize the cases where the right answer is to not use AI at all." Each section ends the same way: not only "when to apply this architecture" but also "when it should not be applied." It is precisely this ability to say a well-grounded "no" to an unsuitable tool, rather than being dazzled by the most powerful one, that the lecture teaches.
