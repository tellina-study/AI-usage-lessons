## Visible slide text

**The visit note.** They listen to the conversation; the text holds the complaints, exam findings, diagnosis, and treatment plan. It's read by the patient themselves and the next physician.

**The discharge summary.** There's nothing to listen to — it's assembled from the progress notes covering the whole hospitalization. It's read by the local physician, the next hospital, and the insurer.

## Speaker notes

The two document forms this whole section moves between are broken down here step by step: the visit note and the discharge summary. They have to be broken down before any talk of tools — without that, it's unclear exactly what the model later intervenes in.

The visit note is made like this. While the patient is talking, the physician holds the structure of the future text in mind: what goes into complaints, what into history, what into exam findings. That's the first action, and it runs in parallel with the live conversation — that's where its whole weight lies. The second action is typing up the note; more often after the visit, between patients, or by the end of the day, and whatever didn't get done goes home with them. The third is proofreading and signing. The signature isn't a formality here: from that minute on, the text becomes what others will act on, and what gets produced if the case is reviewed.

The discharge summary is built differently, and the difference deserves to be stated clearly. There's nothing to listen to there: the patient has been on the ward for a while, and the source is the progress notes for the whole hospitalization, the test results, the medication list. The first action is to assemble a summary from this: what happened, what was done, how it ended, what condition they were discharged in, and what to do next. The second is handing the document to a reader who was nowhere near the hospitalization. The third is producing it if the case is ever reviewed.

Six actions, three per form. Remember this split: later in the section it turns out that the model didn't land in arbitrary spots — it landed in the exact same spot in both chains: the one where the text gets typed up or assembled. Holding the structure in mind, proofreading, and signing all stay with the human in both cases, and so does producing it if the case is reviewed.

And one thing without which what follows will sound like nitpicking. These two forms have different readers and a different cost of error. The visit note is read by the patient themselves and by the next physician at the same clinic — an error there costs a repeated workup or a mistaken continuation of treatment, and the author can still be asked. The discharge summary is read at a different institution, where the author isn't present: treatment gets ordered from it, and there's no one to ask. That's why this section has two case studies, not one, and the second is heavier than the first.

Now something the diagram doesn't draw, but that has to be said. These six actions aren't equal in weight, and the first is the heaviest of all. Holding the structure of the future text in mind during a live conversation is work no one sees and that isn't counted as part of the visit. The patient is talking, the physician is listening, and at the same time sorting what they hear into future fields: this goes under complaints, this under history, this under exam findings. If it doesn't get sorted now, it has to be recalled in the evening, and recall is worse than hearing.

The second thing worth saying about the visit note: it's more often typed up away from the patient, and this has a cost. Time passes between the conversation and the typing — sometimes a whole day of visits — and whatever got forgotten in that gap never makes it into the document. That, incidentally, is where the temptation to carry an earlier note forward wholesale comes from: carrying it forward doesn't require remembering.

One more thing about the discharge summary. It's assembled not from conversation but from what's already written: progress notes, orders, test results, procedure reports. In other words, this is work with someone else's text, long before any model enters — the physician reads what was written over the whole hospitalization, including what colleagues wrote, and merges it into one document. Remember this: when we later say the model assembles the summary from the record, it's doing exactly the work that was already work with text, not with the patient.

And one last, administrative but important point for the accounting. The sixth action — producing it if the case is reviewed — doesn't happen every time, and that's exactly why it gets forgotten when people estimate what documentation actually costs. Its price only becomes visible there: a document that doesn't record an action means, at review, that the action never happened.
