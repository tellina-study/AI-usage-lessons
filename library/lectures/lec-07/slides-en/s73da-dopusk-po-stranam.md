## Visible slide text
**Both orders of work from the previous slide are measured by the rate of procedures with a finding. That same rate also underlies authorization.**

**The quantity is the same across three countries; the consequence diverges.** In the UK, it is used to authorize work in screening. In the US, the very document that set the target level calls applying its own indicators as a condition of authorization a misuse. In Russia a work report is required, but the specialist writes it himself: there is no threshold counted on his behalf.

**We looked and found this in none of the three:** a renewal of authorization that would factor in a physician's measured result **after a period of working with highlighting**.

## Speaker notes

The rate of procedures with a finding — the very quantity used to measure both orders of work without the model — also underlies a physician's authorization to work. And it enters that authorization differently in three countries. In the UK it is a threshold: below fifteen procedures with a finding out of one hundred, authorization for work in the screening program is not granted, and 280 colonoscopies are required before certification, and 100 a year after. In the US, the same quantity is a target level of thirty-five out of one hundred, and the profession's own document explicitly calls applying its own indicators as a condition of authorization a misuse. In Russia it is not counted on the specialist's behalf at all: a work report is required, but he writes it himself.

The question this slide answers follows right from the previous one: who counts this rate, and what happens to a person whose rate is low. The answer diverges across countries so much that you could not guess one jurisdiction from another — and what diverges here is the order of work itself, not the product that carries it out.

Let's start with where the quantity counts toward authorization literally. In the UK, the professional society's guidelines on training and certification in colonoscopy set thresholds on completed work, averaged over three months: reaching the cecum without assistance above ninety out of one hundred procedures, examining the retroflexed view in the rectum above ninety, retrieving what's found above ninety, procedure dissatisfaction below ten out of one hundred — and a rate of procedures with a finding of fifteen out of one hundred and above as the minimum standard for a trainee. Alongside it stands volume: a minimum of two hundred eighty colonoscopies before certification, or two hundred if there is certification in sigmoidoscopy, and no fewer than fifteen procedures over the last three months. After certification the guideline is at least one hundred colonoscopies a year, with a desirable level of one hundred fifty. And the most important point: without certification, a person is not authorized to work in the screening program. That is, the result of his work, counted on his behalf from his own reports, directly decides whether he is let into that work.

Now the US, and here is a finding worth saying slowly. They have the same indicator, and it is set: the target level of the adenoma detection rate (ADR) is thirty-five out of one hundred, and in its own document the profession marks it as an outcome indicator rather than a process one, that is, the strongest grade there is. It is gathered by an industry registry that has accumulated more than ten million colonoscopies. It would seem the basis for authorization is in place. And the very same document states, word for word, that the included indicators and target levels do not reflect a standard of care, authorization requirements, or training standards, and that applying any of this document's indicators that way is a misuse of it. The profession itself forbade turning its own measured indicator into a condition of authorization. Authorization is held up by something else: one hundred points of Maintenance of Certification (MOC) over five years plus a knowledge exam. And the same document calls stripping someone of the right to perform the procedure an extreme measure, not an ordinary outcome of a measurement.

Why this matters for the whole lecture. All evening we've been having a conversation about how a measured number is stronger than an opinion. Here we see the reverse case, and it is not foolishness: a profession that has ten million measured procedures refuses to turn that number into an administrative decision — because the indicator was counted to improve the work; there was never an intent to punish with it, and it has known distortions that, once turned into an authorization criterion, would start driving behavior. This is exactly the kind of judgment the course teaches: a number is fit for the decision it was collected for, and no other.

And Russia. A specialist's periodic accreditation takes place once every five years; the procedure is set by Order No. 709n of the Russian Ministry of Health, dated October 28, 2022. It requires a total of one hundred forty-four hours of continuing-education training — in one course or several, or at least seventy-two hours of courses plus recording on the Continuing Medical Education portal. The second section of the portfolio is a report on professional activity, and it does contain the results of the work performed in the job function. This has to be stated precisely, because the easy and wrong formulation "the result of the work is not looked at" suggests itself here on its own. The correct one sounds different: the specialist draws up the work report himself. There is no numeric threshold on the result — one that would be counted on his behalf and that would block renewal of authorization if not met — in the requirements: not a finding rate, not a withdrawal time. Here, the detection rate is counted only where a center decides on its own to count it; accreditation does not ask for it.

And the slide's last line, one of the reasons it exists at all. We deliberately looked for a jurisdiction where a physician's measured result, after a period of working with highlighting, would factor into renewal of authorization. We looked through the UK and US societies' guidelines, Russia's accreditation regulation, and a consolidated deskilling review. We did not find one in any of the three. This is not a gap in our search that someone will close by tonight: the profession has an indicator for such a measurement, but there is no procedure for it anywhere. So when, two slides from now, we see the measured drop in the rate for the same physicians, keep this in mind: there is nowhere for a physician to learn of that drop in ordinary work, and no order of authorization in these three countries will catch it.
