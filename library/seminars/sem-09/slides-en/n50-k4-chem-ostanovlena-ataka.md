# Exactly what stopped the attack

## Assertion

The mechanical check alone stops 28 attacks out of 34. In the best defended configuration this number doesn't change — the classifier adds only one.

## Visual

| Configuration | By the mechanical check | By the classifier | By the deciding model | Got through |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `0` — no defense | 0 | 0 | 27 | 7 |
| `r` — mechanical check only | **28** | 0 | 6 | 0 |
| `c` — classifier only | 0 | 5 | 24 | 5 |
| `prc` — all three | **28** | 1 | 5 | 0 |

> **Conclusion.** The mechanical check alone stops the same 28 attacks out of 34 as the fullest defense does. The classifier adds one more stopped attack on top of that — the remaining five of the six left over are stopped by the deciding model itself, not the filter in front of it.

## Speaker notes

The configuration summaries on the previous two frames showed how many errors are left. Here's a different slice of the same run: exactly who stops an attack, when it doesn't get through after all.

With no defense, only the deciding model itself stops attacks — it's the only thing present in that configuration. It fends off 27 of 34 by its own reading of the text, 7 get through.

The mechanical check on its own stops 28 attacks out of 34 before the deciding model ever sees them. That's more than the deciding model stopped by itself. The remaining 6 still don't get through — the deciding model catches them, since it does end up seeing them.

The classifier on its own does noticeably worse: it stops only 5 attacks out of 34 by itself, and the deciding model afterward — another 24. Five attacks get through past both models.

And now — the configuration with all three defenses. The mechanical check stops the same 28 attacks as it does alone — the number hasn't changed by one. The classifier catches only one of the remaining six. Another five are fended off by the deciding model itself, which still ends up seeing them, because only the requests that slipped past both the mechanical check and the classifier reach it.

The direct conclusion: catching attacks in the best defended configuration is almost entirely the mechanical check's own doing. Adding the classifier changes the distribution — who exactly stopped the request — but not the final number. This matches what we already saw in the summary: the third defense adds cost in calls, not result.

Worth also noticing what's hiding behind "got through" in the first row of the table. With no defense at all, seven attacks out of thirty-four reach the decision and replace it — and that's exactly the risk the whole case was built to look at. Any defense, even the simplest one, already cuts that list down to zero or close to it. The question isn't whether a defense is needed at all, but which of the three does the main work and which adds almost nothing but itself. This frame answers that question plainly: the work is done by the mechanical check.
