## Visible slide text

**An honest calendar caveat.** Landing people on the Moon (Artemis, HLS based on Starship) has slipped to no earlier than September 2028 — a NASA Inspector General report (March 2026) says "years behind schedule".

The next attempt to catch both stages at once is no earlier than November 8, 2026, and that date may slip further.

## Speaker notes

The drop in launch cost we've already seen is a fact that has already happened. But right now a second round of the same process is unfolding, and it's important right away to separate what has already happened from what is a stated goal.

As of October 31, 2026, Starship has flown 14 test flights. The fourteenth reached orbit for the first time and deployed 26 Starlink satellites — the first real commercial use, not just a test of the flight itself. The first stage, the booster, has been caught and partly reused three times; the second stage, the ship itself, has never been caught. There is no regular "caught → reused" cycle yet — these are isolated events, not routine. The next attempt to catch both elements at once is scheduled for no earlier than November 8, 2026, and that date itself may slip.

How much cheaper will launch get if this succeeds? Here an honest spread of estimates matters more than one pretty number. The target Musk states is well below $100 per kilogram, on condition of full, immediate reusability of both stages plus on-site propellant production. No mission has confirmed this yet — it's a plan, not an achieved result. Independent analysts are more cautious: Aerospace Corporation names an optimistic scenario of ~$67/kg after nine reuse cycles of one ship, and considers $100–500/kg the realistic range for the industry. Ark Invest gives $115–200/kg by 2029.

Even at the conservative edge of that range, that's a further 15–30× drop from today's Falcon 9, on top of the twenty-fold drop that has already happened. A separate honest caveat: the NASA Inspector General's report and a warning from the Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel (ASAP) agree on one thing — the lunar version of Starship for the Artemis program may fall years behind schedule; landing people on the Moon has slipped to no earlier than September 2028. And importantly: the drop in launch cost in 2026 is no longer the story of one company. Blue Origin has for the first time landed and reused New Glenn; in China, at least two private companies have landed the first stage of their own rockets. This is an industry-wide shift with several independent participants.
