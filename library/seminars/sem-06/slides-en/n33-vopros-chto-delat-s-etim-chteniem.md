# What to do with all that reading

## Assertion

Who here, in the past month, had an edit where finding the places turned out to cost more than fixing them? How many files did you have to open to find the ones you needed? Here there are four places and forty files: pick a card and say what it wins by.

## Visual

> **Here there are four places and forty files. Pick a card — and say out loud what it wins by.**

`read all forty files yourself, in this same session` · `open a second session, ask there and carry the list over by hand` · `call in a colleague who has worked with the pricing plans for a long time` · `hand it to a subagent: it reads in its own context and brings back the list of places` · `edit from memory, without searching`

## Speaker notes

It is worth starting with an edit of your own. Who, in the past month, had an edit where finding the places turned out to cost more than fixing them — and how many files did you have to open to find the ones you needed? Almost everyone who has worked in someone else's project has an edit like that, and the number of files named works better from then on than any example.

The question of the case rests on exactly that: what to edit is known, where to edit sits in forty files, of which four are needed. There are five cards: read all forty files yourself, in this same session; open a second session, ask there and carry the list over by hand; call in a colleague who has worked with the pricing plans for a long time; hand it to a subagent — it reads in its own context and brings back the list of places; edit from memory, without searching.

Picking a card is half the work. The other half is saying what it wins by against the other four. The grounds weigh more here than the choice: on the next screen the count of files tests precisely the reason the card was taken for.

Not many answers get said to a question like this, and each one is worth going through.

**"A subagent — because it is faster."** The usual first guess, and it comes to almost everyone. Say it to yourself in full — "a separate worker will read forty files faster than I will" — and keep it ready. The next screen tests it by counting, and it does not pass the test. The breakdown, read without the guess having been said, stays a report of a fact; with the guess, it changes the picture, and this case rests on exactly that.

**"A second session."** The option that occurs first to anyone who works in two tabs; it is worth taking seriously. One thing has to be added to it: what happens after the answer has been received. The answer to that is almost always the same — "I'll carry it over." That word is what gets examined further on: carrying it over is done by a person, and it costs time and precision.

**"A colleague."** The only option able to answer in a minute, if the colleague is fresh on the subject, and the only one that depends on somebody else's schedule. It is not in the list for completeness: in live work this is a frequent and frequently correct move.

**The answer that is not on the cards** — "ask whoever wrote this", "look in the commit history", "write the question down as a skill for later". Such answers are legitimate: the cards set the field of choice, they are not its boundary. The commit history, for instance, will narrow the search if the rule changed through explicit edits with intelligible messages, and will not help if it crept outward through refactorings.

Picking two cards is allowed too, and in live work that is what people do: first a subagent, then a short conversation with whoever wrote those migrations. In that case it is worth naming which of the two comes first and why — the order changes the cost here.

If no edit of your own comes to mind, it is worth choosing from the scene on the previous screen — and then checking separately how much the choice depends on the edit and the search sitting in the same session.
