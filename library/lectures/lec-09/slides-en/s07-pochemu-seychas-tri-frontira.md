## Visible slide text

A shared pattern: the drop in launch cost **does not solve the economics** of each track on its own — it lowers the threshold for even attempting it.

## Speaker notes

Three tracks — space-based solar power, lunar and Martian bases, resource mining on asteroids — are not new ideas: all three were discussed in engineering literature back in the 1970s. The question worth asking explicitly: what changed right now, turning them from speculative constructs into the subject of real investment decisions?

Space-based solar power rests on the same threshold as all of Section 4: the arrival of reusable launch systems lowered the cost of orbital access by an order of magnitude. At today's commercial launch price, electricity from space-based power, by analysts' estimate, costs 5–10 times more than ground-based alternatives — a threshold, not an achieved state. But interest is already material: on April 27, 2026, Overview Energy and Meta announced an agreement for access to future capacity of up to 1 gigawatt, with a demonstration in 2028 and commercial delivery in 2030.

Lunar bases: a barrier that stopped being a state monopoly. Before 2024, not a single Moon landing had ever been carried out by a commercial organization. On March 2, 2025, Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost Mission 1 spacecraft made the first fully successful landing on the Moon by a private organization. This is a structural, not a quantitative, shift: landing on the Moon stopped being the exclusive competence of a handful of national agencies. This doesn't cancel the honest caveat about NASA's unadjusted $100 billion estimate for landing six astronauts on Mars — a commercial landing of a small uncrewed craft and a large crewed base remain tasks of a different order of magnitude.

Resource mining on asteroids is the narrowest frontier of the three: the drop in launch cost lowered the threshold for attempting it, but didn't solve the fundamental economic question. So far, no one has mined and sold material from an asteroid commercially. AstroForge's first mission ("Odin") launched on February 26, 2025 and was declared unsuccessful in early March of the same year; its second, as of October 31, 2026, has not yet launched — it's stated for the fourth quarter of 2026. The shared pattern across all three: the drop in cost lowers the threshold for attempting it, but doesn't solve the track's economics on its own, and the speed of the transition from "speculative idea" to "testable question" differs for each one.
