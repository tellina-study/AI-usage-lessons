## Speaker notes

The industry name for this modality is moving image, and underneath it lives a production chain built more intricately than sound's. It makes sense to start the breakdown with the decision this chain makes, and with where the expensive point sits in it: everything else in the section reads relative to these two things.

The stage's decision is short to state — exactly what the viewer will see in every second of screen time. Behind this decision sits the most expensive chain of obligations in the whole chapter: music rights, rights to faces on screen, location rights, union rates, insurance, client approvals. So the cheapness of a single frame does not translate into the cheapness of a finished spot anywhere close to one-to-one, and that gap is exactly what the section's three breakdowns are about.

The classical chain works with footage already shot — an array of shots from which the editor picks the ones that make it in. The ratio of footage shot to footage released is a professional quantity with its own name and a known order of magnitude: around ten to one in scripted advertising, tens to one in documentary. The question the stage answers is: which shot makes it into the edit. The unit of account on set is the shooting day and the shot — a continuous piece of footage from the camera turning on to turning off.

The generative chain works with a description and reference frames. The input is text, sometimes plus a starting frame, sometimes plus a set of references to lock in a character's look — current systems take up to ten reference frames and up to fifteen references per generation. The output is a clip from four to twenty seconds long. The question stays the same — which shot makes it into the edit — and that is the main point: the selection stage hasn't disappeared, it has grown.

The difference between the two chains reads conveniently off where the expensive point sits in each. In the classical chain, producing the shot is expensive: the shift, lighting, performers, location permits; selection, meanwhile, is cheap — the editor reviews the footage and picks. In the generative chain, selecting is expensive: producing a shot costs cents, while reviewing several hundred clips, checking character consistency across them, and assembling a coherent scene out of the survivors costs person-days. The ratio of trials to usable output grows by an order to an order-and-a-half against the mode generation is imitating. The scarcity has moved from one end of the chain to the other, and the whole economics of the section is about that.

From this follows the order in which to read the numbers that come next. Any message of the form "the spot cost two thousand dollars instead of forty thousand" describes the cost of producing the shot and says nothing about the cost of selection; any message of the form "five people made the campaign" describes the number of people performing one operation and says nothing about the number of people involved in the work. Both types of message will show up in the section, both will be counted against the classical mode, and both will have to be read with an adjustment for where the expensive part moved to.

The practical conclusion for an engineer choosing a tool for the task: before planning savings on shot production, estimate what selection will cost. If the task is such that selection is done by a person and done by eye, the gain on production runs straight into human throughput — and after that it no longer matters how much a single generation costs.

The section's three breakdowns are arranged to test this conclusion at three different scales. Chinese micro-dramas — industrial production, where the unit of account is the release, and the comparison runs against live-action shooting of the same format. A prediction-market ad for the NBA Finals — one person's work, where the classical mode is counted twice, by the American slot's budget and by a Russian baseline band. A major brand's Christmas campaign — large-scale production, where what's counted is the number of people and the timeline: the budget there is not disclosed. In all three cases the gain is counted against the classical mode, and in none of them against zero.
