## Speaker notes

The room's expectation is almost always "fewer," and this error is systemic. The message "five specialists made the ad" gets retold as "five replaced fifty," even though the five were the ones pressing the button, while the other ninety-five didn't go anywhere: coordinating three contractors, reconciling material produced by four different methods, legal review, music, market adaptation.

Messages like this have to be checked with one question: does it name the number of people performing an operation, or the number of people involved in the work? These are two different numbers, and a press release almost always states the first while it gets read as the second. The question carries over anywhere: "two engineers replaced a department," "one analyst does the work of five" — almost always this is about how many people carry out one specific operation, not about how many people it takes to bring the work to release.

What actually changed deserves to be stated precisely, because the change is enormous and shouldn't be understated. The timeline: a year against a month, a twelvefold difference by production cycle, and this is the one figure confirmed by the company's own leadership. The number of attempts: one shoot against over seventy thousand generated clips. This is the real content of the change: a collapse in the price of an attempt, and in the time it takes to make one. No freeing-up of people happened here.

And from this follows the reason the slide exists at all. If the number of people didn't fall while the price of an attempt fell by three orders of magnitude, then people are doing something different from what they did before. What, exactly, is visible in the makeup of the hundred: selection, reconciliation, coordination, rights clearance, adaptation. The work moved from production to selection and assembly, and that is exactly the answer this section gives to the lecture's question, in its own units.

It's also worth holding on to what this slide doesn't claim. It doesn't claim that generative production never cuts headcount anywhere — it claims that in this one, most thoroughly documented case, it didn't. You can't generalize from one case in either direction: not "headcount will fall," and not "headcount will always stay the same." The honest formulation: check any stated number of people with the question above, and count the industry-wide outcome by employment figures that none of this campaign's participants have published.

It's worth taking apart where this very expectation error comes from, because it will recur in any conversation about automation. A press release describes an operation: how many people did what a large team used to do. The reader fills in the missing piece: if five people do the operation, the rest aren't needed. That inference feels logical and is almost always wrong, because the operation is only part of the work. Around it stand specification, coordination, rights, adaptation, and sign-off, and not one of those parts disappears just because the operation got cheaper. In the case broken down here, it shows up in the numbers directly: five pressed the button, a hundred people made the campaign.

A second observation in the same vein concerns the timeline. Shrinking the cycle from a year to a month isn't just faster — it's a different way of making decisions. On a year-long cycle, the client is forced to guess what will be relevant a year out, and a wrong guess costs the entire production. On a month-long cycle, you can start later, once conditions are known more precisely, and redo it if it doesn't land. That's why shrinking the timeline is more valuable to the client than simple cost reduction: it removes the need to guess.

And a third, directly engineering point. Where the number of people didn't change but the number of attempts did, expect a growing load on selection and assembly. Seventy thousand clips have to be viewed, sorted, checked against each other for character, lighting, and color, consolidated into a single sequence, and cleared for rights. This is work that didn't exist with one shoot: there, selection ran across a handful of takes of one shot. The load hasn't disappeared and hasn't shrunk — it moved, and it should be counted in person-days of viewing, not in the cost of generation.
